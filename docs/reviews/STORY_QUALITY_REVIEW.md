# Story quality review and four improvement plans

Review of the World Bible, its Story Reader and the review notes on
`GetPutTaco` at `d988149`, 3 November 2026. Passage IDs (`SR-…`) refer to
[WORLD_BIBLE_STORY_READER.md](../WORLD_BIBLE_STORY_READER.md); decision IDs
(`WB-…`) refer to [WORLD_BIBLE.md](../WORLD_BIBLE.md).

This is a craft review, not an approval of proposed lore. It recommends
changes, including changes to established canon, for the author to accept or
reject. No game text or data was changed.

**Brief used for this review.** The audience is all ages, with a literary
story worth following. The top priorities are mystery and lore, and how well
the story fits the gameplay. Canon may change if the story improves.

## 1. Verdict

The story has a strong heart and a missing brain. The second-half scenes are
the best writing in the project: concrete, restrained and humane. The
first-half antagonist text is generic, the central mystery has no answer
behind it, and the falling-block magic, the game's main activity, barely
touches the story.

**Overall: 6 / 10.** The foundations are sound enough that every plan below
builds on what exists. None of them requires starting again.

| Dimension                   | Score | Summary                                                                                   |
| --------------------------- | ----- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Structure                   | 7     | Clear monomyth spine and a real midpoint false victory; the second half repeats one beat. |
| Supporting characters       | 8     | Rowan, Odile, Ketter, Irdrath and the mentors are specific and well observed.             |

## 2. What works

Keep these. Each plan below protects them.

- **Character through action.** Odile's packed bag (`SR-archiveRefuge-01`),
  Ketter's cooling soup (`bellShift`), and Rowan asking whether Pete has charged
  for the room (`rowanReturn`) carry emotion without speeches. That last line is
  the best in the game.
- **A grounded theme.** The underground conflict is care against control.
  Irdrath keeps sleepers warm but will not let them leave. The Lishal feed
  people and then lock the doors. This is a mature theme told in terms a child
  understands: who is allowed to decide what is good for you?
- **A genuine midpoint.** The Wyrm "closes its eye and sleeps, a real sleep
  this time" (`SR-wyrmFall-03`). Turning the monster into a tired animal and
  then hearing the voice say "I have others" is an excellent reversal.
- **Distinct mentors.** Torva's "spark", Hazel's "sprout" and Rook's impatience
  give seven teachers seven voices in very few words.
- **An ending at human scale.** Hareguy asking for breakfast and Rowan's knee
  catching as he stands (`mossvaleRecovery`) is the right kind of last image.
- **Discipline in the bible.** The Established / Team source / Proposed labels
  and the continuity checks will stop future writers contradicting canon.

## 3. Findings

Ordered by impact on the brief.

### F1. The mystery has no answer behind it

The voice is the reason for every event, yet nobody, including the author,
knows what it is. The bible says the relationship between the stones,
moonlight, blight and sleep "still needs a consistent explanation" (section 4),
and the voice's nature is open (WB-Q02). Several basic questions have no
answer:

- Why does the voice want people asleep? What does it gain from sleepers?
- Why do the Lishal impose sleep? Do they serve the voice, or is it a
  coincidence of goals?
- What is Irdrath, and why does the Firstcoal matter?
- What are the seven stones, and why does the violet stone fail first?
- Why does the ward contain the voice, and who built it?

A mystery only satisfies when the author knows the answer and plants fair
clues. At the moment, the clues are atmosphere. The visions contradict each
other ("the moon above is just my pale reflection" against "I drank the
moonlight"), and the bible rightly warns not to treat them as fact. That leaves
players nothing to solve.

### F2. The magic system and the story are strangers

Players spend most of their time clearing rows with seven schools. The story
mentions this only in Pip's tower and lesson flavour text. Strong patterns are
already present but unused:

- seven schools, seven stones, seven mentors, seven Lishal, seven crystal
  sockets, and the Malificat "drawing on all seven";
- a sleep plague, but no sleep mechanic on the board;
- Weaving, which combines schools in an order the player chooses, unlocks after
  the Wyrm, exactly when the story turns to cooperation, yet the rite does not
  use it;
- an attitude system with Hostile to Devoted branches for Vesper that has no
  effect on the ending.

### F3. Two voices, two games

The first-half antagonist text is stock dark fantasy. There are about thirty
interchangeable Vesper barks ("surface-bug", "brave meat burns in the Wyrm's
throat", "dust returns to dust"), and the Wyrm says "I am the silence between
stars". The second half is quiet and precise. The same Vesper who says "You're
blocking my light, surface-bug" later says "I read every one". That reads as a
different character, and the bible's own rule says the final form must not
become "an unrelated villain wearing the same name" (section 5).

### F4. Vesper's turn is underpowered

Vesper is the emotional centre of the second half, but before the midpoint
they are a taunting NPC whose motive is "seeking glory". The Fennick connection
first appears in a single line after the Wyrm. When Vesper chooses the voice
(`vesperAcceptance`), the player has no strong picture of what Vesper wants
badly enough to accept a fate they know killed earlier vessels. "I can hear the
surface" is a lovely line with nothing behind it.

### F5. The hero's arc stops at the refusal

Each hero gets a clear hesitation (`SR-refusal-01`): Fern will not leave the
sleepers unwatched, Lance will not leave his post, Ares trusts only books, and
Vex says it is not their problem. None of these returns. From the archive on,
every hero line is the shared "Chosen hero" text, such as "Where do you need
me?". The monomyth table lists "Resurrection", but nothing in the hero changes.

### F6. The second half repeats one beat

From `archiveRefuge` to `ascentLanding`, the hero mostly fixes infrastructure:
a drain, a channel, a tram, a lift, a bell circuit, a shutter brake and a
jacked beam. Each scene is good alone. Together they flatten the rhythm and
crowd out wonder, discovery and danger. The surface mentor approaches share the
same "help X fix Y" pattern, so the whole game risks a single texture.

### F7. Leftover authoring text and continuity slips

These appear in player-facing strings and are quick fixes:

| Passage                 | Issue                                                                                                                                 |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `SR-chapterEnd-02`      | "After the Moon Wyrm falls, Captain Vale has the last word:" reads as a writer's note. Vale's line has a comma splice.                |
| `SR-chapterEnd-03`      | "The Sunken Ruins' seal then glows, distracting everyone." Sunken Ruins is an obsolete map name (`src/data/maps.ts`, `story.ts:337`). |
| `SR-chapterEnd-04`      | "Suddenly a voice is heard" is passive and flat at the biggest moment in the game.                                                    |
| `SR-chapterEnd-06`      | "I taught the boy who made them to walk" conflicts with Vesper's they/them pronouns everywhere else.                                  |
| The ward after the Wyrm | "It will open in the next chapter" speaks to the player out of the fiction.                                                           |
| `malificatRisen`        | Speaker label is lowercase `malificat`; the bible spells it Malificat.                                                                |
| Visions 1–3             | Physical claims ("every breath… draws air from my cavern") with no later payoff or contradiction.                                     |

### F8. Jargon in place of understanding

The bible itself flags voice branch, relay, return, ring, ward and station
(section 10). Young players will not follow "upper branch carries the voice,
lower one feeds our lamps". The return procedure is a technical object the
player copies rather than a discovery they understand.

### F9. Name tone needs a pass

Most names suit a gentle fantasy (Mossvale, Kestrel, Odile). A few break the
register: "Hareguy" can read as a joke ("hairy guy") at the most sincere moment
in the opening, and "Steve the Cave Hermit" feels out of place. If either is
deliberate humour, keep it consistent. Otherwise rename both early, before
voice or art depends on them.

### F10. The bible says what the story is not, more than what it is

The bible is excellent governance, but much of it is negation: "does not",
"must not", "is not". It has no theme statement, tone pillars, or secret
history for writers to aim at. A new writer learns what to avoid, not what to
create.

## 4. Four improvement plans

The plans are independent and can be combined. Each states its goal, the main
work, the risks and the effort. Effort is rough writer-weeks for one
experienced writer, excluding engineering and art.

### Plan A — Polish and unify (one voice, one game)

**Goal.** Make the existing story read as one confident work without changing
its structure. Lowest risk, fastest result.

**Work.**

1. Fix every item in F7. Rewrite `chapterEnd` as staged fiction: Vale's line,
   then the ward flaring, then the voice. Remove "Sunken Ruins" and the
   out-of-fiction chapter line. Correct the pronoun and speaker label.
2. Rewrite the first-half Vesper barks, visions and Wyrm prelude in the
   second-half register. Cut the thirty barks to about twelve. Give each a
   specific observation about the hero's last action, which is how the later
   Vesper talks ("You took your time").
3. Pay off the four refusals. Give each hero one alternative line in three
   late scenes (`vesperAcceptance`, `campaignRite`, `mossvaleRecovery`) that
   answers their hesitation. Lance leaves a post to hold the shutter brake.
   Vex says "not my problem" to Vesper and means the opposite.
4. Replace jargon with physical names a child can picture: "the voice pipe"
   and "the lamp pipe" rather than branches, and "the missing piece of the
   circle" rather than "lower return".
5. Add a one-page **story heart** section to the bible: theme ("Care that
   will not let go becomes control"), three tone pillars, and a short
   paragraph on what the ending should make a player feel.

**Risks.** It leaves F1, F2 and F6 untouched, so the story is cleaner but not
deeper.

**Effort.** About 2 weeks.

**Done when.** A cold reader cannot tell which half was written first. Every
hero hesitation has a visible answer.

### Plan B — The answer behind the voice (mystery and lore)

**Goal.** Give the world a secret history the author knows, then plant a fair
clue trail from the first scene so the midpoint and ending feel inevitable.
This plan answers the first priority in the brief.

**Work.**

1. **Write the secret history first.** Keep it to two pages, author-only, and
   label it Established once approved. One candidate shows the level of detail
   needed; it is a proposal, not a requirement:
   - The seven stones once sang together and kept the world awake: the
     lights lit, the water moving and the seasons turning. Each school of
     magic is one stone's voice, which is why there are seven.
   - Beneath them lies an **eighth stone**, cracked and buried long ago
     because its song was sleep. It is not evil. It wants rest for everything,
     forever, and calls that mercy. That is the voice.
   - It cannot move, so it speaks through **vessels**: the Wyrm, earlier
     bearers in Odile's ledger, and finally Vesper. Sleepers are not food.
     Each one is a listener, and with enough listeners its song can drown out
     the other seven.
   - The **Lishal** are the eighth stone's old choir. They truly believe sleep
     is kindness, which explains why they care for people and imprison them.
     **Irdrath** is a failed warden: a fire spirit set to keep sleepers warm
     until help came, who forgot that help was the point.
   - **Rowan's well** cracked the bedrock above the eighth stone. The violet
     stone, its nearest neighbour, failed first. His line "Cracked the bedrock
     with it" (`SR-refusal-04`) becomes the first clue, and his guilt becomes a
     real arc that ends when he helps cut the wedges for the rite.
   - The **ward** is a silent circle built by Fennick's order. "Ves, en." is in
     the old stone-language, which gives the phrase a real meaning (WB-Q07).
2. **Build a clue ledger** in the bible: a table of each fact, the scene that
   plants it, and the scene that confirms it. Aim for three plants per major
   reveal, at least one of them in the first hour.
3. **Make the visions consistent.** Each vision tells a partial truth from the
   voice's point of view. Players who reread them after the ending should find
   that the voice never lied, only framed things.
4. **Seed Fennick and Vesper before the midpoint.** Fennick appears once in
   Brightwater, lantern in hand, asking about a traveller in a dark cloak. A
   resident at Moonfall remembers a child who followed the old man around.
5. **Resolve WB-Q02 and WB-Q05** directly from the secret history, and update
   continuity checks WB-C09 and WB-C14.

**Risks.** Changes canon (Rowan's role, the stones' origin). Over-explaining
can kill wonder, so reveal the history through objects and conversations and
never through a lecture. Some players will never learn all of it, which is
fine.

**Effort.** About 3–4 weeks, mostly design. It then makes Plans C and D easier.

**Done when.** The author can answer every question in F1 in one sentence, and
a playtester can guess the voice's nature before the rite.

### Plan C — Play the story (the story lives on the board)

**Goal.** Make the falling-block battles express the plot and theme so the
player feels the story through their hands. This plan answers the second
priority in the brief.

**Work.**

1. **Sleep as a board effect.** Encounters touched by the voice add "drowsy"
   blocks that slowly grey out neighbours and earn no mana. They teach the
   plague's threat before any character explains it. Nature and Ice already
   produce colourless material, so the rules have a precedent.
2. **One school per stone, one stone per mentor.** Make the link explicit. When
   a mentor teaches a school, their stone visibly brightens on the overworld.
   The seven-stone crisis becomes a progress bar the player fills by learning.
3. **The rite is a Weaving.** The final ritual becomes a Weaving puzzle built
   from the schools the player actually learned. Bonded mentors each supply a
   component, which uses the existing `roadBack` lines. Weaving then means
   magic made together, which is the story's answer to the voice's single song.
   Keep the WB-C15 fallback: trained helpers fill missing schools.
4. **Bosses that argue through mechanics.**
   - **Irdrath** heats rows to keep them from clearing: protection that
     prevents progress. Breaking the Firstcoal releases them.
   - **The Lishal** sing seven colours that lull rows asleep. Silencing a
     singer clears one colour, so the player meets them as individuals.
   - **Vesper's duel** mirrors the player's own spell order, as a rival who
     studied you.
   - **The Malificat** draws from all seven stones. Each stone that bonded
     mentors helped to light resists the draw.
5. **Let Vesper's attitude matter.** The Hostile to Devoted branches set what
   Vesper says when they refuse the bridge and in the recovery scene. A
   Devoted Vesper takes Fennick's cup of water. The ending choice stays the
   same; only its emotional colour changes.
6. **Pip's tower as a frame.** Pip's tower is already the first puzzle. Return
   to it at each act break with one new tier, so the final "another tier"
   image means something.

**Risks.** Needs engineering and balance work, and must respect the current
casting contract in `BOARD_MAGIC_PLAN.md`. Drowsy blocks must stay readable
and fair. A playtest gate is needed before story text depends on any mechanic.

**Effort.** About 2 weeks of writing and design, plus engineering estimated
separately.

**Done when.** A player can describe the story's theme by talking only about
how the battles felt.

### Plan D — Vesper's story (character-led restructure)

**Goal.** Rebuild the emotional spine as a triangle of the hero, Vesper and
Fennick, and fix the second-half pacing at the same time.

**Work.**

1. **Give Vesper a want a child understands.** Vesper was the quiet child
   nobody chose, and Fennick looked after many children. The voice was the
   first thing that spoke only to them. Their want is to be heard. That makes
   "I can hear the surface" the climax of their arc, and makes Fennick's
   failure to reach them a real cost.
2. **Introduce Vesper as a rival, not a villain.** Before the midpoint, Vesper
   competes with the hero for the same mentors' approval and sometimes helps.
   Taunts become challenges. Their Master is a secret the player uncovers
   rather than a word they repeat.
3. **Give the hero something to lose.** At the bridge, Vesper's refusal echoes
   the hero's own hesitation from the opening. The class lines from Plan A
   become the hero's answer to Vesper, in their own voice.
4. **Fix the underground rhythm.** Merge the seven repair beats into three, and
   put the freed space into:
   - one place of wonder, such as the Glass Warrens seen lit for the first
     time;
   - one scene of real danger with no repair solution;
   - one quiet Vesper scene with no conflict, where the two simply read the
     ledger together. The current `rootwaysRecord` is close to this.
5. **Strengthen the ending.** Keep Vesper alive and under guard. Add one small,
   earned gesture between the hero and Vesper, shaped by Plan C's attitude
   state. The young audience needs to see that a person can be stopped without
   being thrown away.

**Risks.** Large rewrite of second-half scenes that are already playable.
Making Vesper sympathetic must not excuse what they did; the existing "no one
asks the people Vesper harmed to forgive them" line keeps that boundary.

**Effort.** About 4–6 weeks, plus rework of quest gates and encounters.

**Done when.** Playtesters name Vesper as the character they remember, and
can say what Vesper wanted.

## 5. Recommendation

Do the plans in this order:

1. **Plan A now.** It is cheap, removes the visible errors, and every other
   plan depends on a single voice.
2. **Plan B next.** The secret history is the foundation for both priorities.
   Plan C's mechanics and Plan D's Vesper need to know what the voice is.
3. **Plan C alongside B.** Prototype drowsy blocks and the Weaving rite while
   the lore is written, so the two inform each other.
4. **Plan D last, if playtests show the ending lacks impact.** Parts of it,
   such as seeding Fennick and restoring the hero's voice, are already covered
   by Plans A and B.

Together, A + B + C reach the brief: a mystery worth solving that the player
solves partly by playing. Treat Plan D as the upgrade from good to memorable.
