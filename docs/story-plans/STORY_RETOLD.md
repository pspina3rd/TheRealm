# Mana Crystals — The Story Retold

This is the whole story of Mana Crystals told as one story, from the first dark night to the last
breakfast. Part 1 tells the story as it is in the game today. Part 2 shows how a few scenes would
read with a fuller version of Vesper and Fennick.

## Part 1 — The story as it stands

This retelling follows **Fern**, the Ranger, who begins in Mossvale. The other three heroes live
through the same events with a different hometown and a different reason to stay put: Lance, the
Knight, in Cairnwick, feels his duty is to hold his post; Ares, the Mage, in Merehaven, trusts his
books; Vex, the Rogue, in Bramblecross, insists none of it is their problem.

### 1. The sleeping houses

Sevenstones was a land kept by seven crystals, and on the night the story begins, all seven glowed
in a ring around it. Then the violet one went dark, and the other six dimmed. Far beneath the
stones something enormous breathed in, and every lamp in every village dimmed at once. A voice too
deep to hear, only to feel, said one word: _"Sleep."_

At dawn in Mossvale, Fern lit the lane lamps as usual. Bean was out tending her herb beds. Ordor
was opening his stall. But three houses stayed shuttered, and nobody inside them would wake. Fern
noticed small wrong things first, as a ranger would: "The baker's never slept through a dawn in his
life. And the wind hasn't moved since midnight."

At the well, Elder Rowan hauled up a bucket. The water in it was violet. "Three houses still abed,
and the well's gone purple," he said. "Something's wrong, kid."

The morning turned into a round of neighbourly jobs. Rowan's knees would no longer take the ladder,
so Fern went down and cleared the mould from the well, which taught Fern the earth spell Quake.
Bean needed her blighted beds replanted. Pip, a small boy whose crystal tower had jammed, needed it
fixed before supper or there would be cabbage instead of pudding. Ordor would sell, and haggle, but
he still had to make a living. The water ran clear again. The sleeping houses stayed silent.

### 2. Rowan's request

Fern stood at the clean well and looked down the road toward Brightwater, and didn't want to go.
"Those houses need someone watching over them."

Then Kestrel the courier dropped out of the wind with two letters. One was from Selene at Moonfall,
addressed "to whoever's still awake in Mossvale". The other was for Rowan, from the inn at
Brightwater. He read it twice, and his hands shook.

"My boy, Hareguy. Asleep three weeks at the inn, and they can't wake him. I can't make that road on
these knees, kid."

He pressed an old rune stone into Fern's palm, the one he had found the year he dug the well and
used to crack the bedrock. "Take it, kid." Rowan had once wondered aloud whether digging that well
had let something out. The game never says whether he did.

So Fern went, not to save the world, but to check on one sleeping son for a man who could not walk
the distance.

### 3. The surface journey

As soon as Fern set out, the visions began. A sky turned black inside Fern's head, and a voice
mocked the work of patching wells: "Do you truly think you can mend a world that is falling asleep?"
It grew more direct the farther Fern went. "Dig deeper, break the stone. Every rock you shift brings
your heart closer to my coil." Who or what was speaking, the visions never said.

The road led through lived-in country, and each region had a teacher who had a trouble of their
own. Torva at the Forgefyre forge was losing the steadiness of her hands. Maren watched her lake
cloud over at Glasswater. Rook chased storms at the Stormcrown Peaks that no longer behaved. Hazel
fought the rot in the Thornwood meadows. Dorn at the Redrock Quarry had lost a mining crew in a
collapse and taught so nobody would dig blind again. Kestrel carried letters along the Windward
Cliffs, and Selene at Moonfall tried to understand why the moonlight was failing. Fern learned
their magic in whatever order the road allowed, usually by helping with a local problem first.

In Brightwater, Pete the innkeeper told it plainly: "Rowan's boy is upstairs. Three weeks asleep.
Breathes fine. Just won't wake." Captain Vale's ward lamps at the cave mouth were dying, and once
Fern helped light them, Vale wrote Fern a pass into the Moon Caves.

Along the way a cloaked figure kept appearing: Vesper, who called the power below "the Master" and
seemed to be watching Fern on its behalf. Vesper was scornful ("You're blocking my light,
surface-bug"), set puzzles as tests, and now and then let something slip: "I too once walked in the
sun. Then I heard the serpent's whisper under the earth." Vesper had once gone looking for glory in
ancient places, and had bowed to what they found there. Even so, a good showing from Fern could
draw a grudging "You continue to surprise me."

### 4. The Moon Wyrm

Deep in the Moon Caves, a vast serpent lay coiled around the moon-crystal, its breath like frost.
It spoke with the voice from the visions: "I am the silence between stars. Show me why your brief
life deserves the dawn."

Fern fought it and won. The coil slackened, and the Wyrm's last word broke off halfway, as if
whatever had been speaking had simply stopped. What was left was only an animal: grey, ancient,
half-blind. It let out a long, shaking breath, closed its eye, and slept, "a real sleep this time."

Moonlight poured up through the stone. Across Sevenstones the violet stone flickered and glowed. In
Brightwater, sleepers stirred and yawned. Captain Vale began to thank Fern: "You came back, the
realm owes you; so do I..."

He never finished. The ancient ward at the old ruins flared silver, and the voice came again,
rumbling through the breeze, with no Wyrm to carry it this time. The grass flattened and the lamps
guttered. "A good throat. Old, and very obedient. **I have others.**"

At the cave mouth, Vale's guards found a dark cloak, empty and soaked. Wet footprints led across
Sevenstones to the glowing ward. Beside it waited an old man holding a brass lantern with no flame.
He looked at the prints, then at Fern. "Those prints. I'd know them anywhere. I taught the boy who
made them to walk."

The victory was real, but it was not the end. Fern had beaten the creature the voice spoke
through, not the voice itself.

### 5. The archive

"Vesper?" Fern asked.

"Yes. I'm Fennick. I looked after them when they were small."

The prints went straight into the stone. Fennick found a service entrance, and together they
shifted a fallen slab to uncover a narrow stair beside the ward. Fennick reached for the iron
handhold, but Vale asked who would keep the entrance open if both of them went, and Fern asked him
to stay. His hand stayed on the iron a moment longer. Then he let go. "If you find Vesper — tell
them I'm here."

Below lay a flooded archive. Odile was keeping a refuge there for sleeping residents, wrapping
blankets around them with her own travel bag packed and waiting by her boots. She meant to leave as
soon as the lift came down, but the water was over the lower catch and she couldn't clear the drain
and hold the sluice at once. Fern took the dry side and lifted. The landing drained; the lift came
down; the first loaded stretcher could go up. "That's one trip we can make. Thank you."

Odile asked Fern to carry an old ledger with them. It recorded the channels below, and something
else as well: earlier people who had carried the voice. It listed the rewards they were promised,
the dates they died, and the orders appointing their replacements. The voice had used bearers
before. Beating one would not stop it from finding another.

### 6. Irdrath

The road went through the cistern works, where an ash figure with a red glow pulsing under his ribs
stood among the steaming pipes. This was Irdrath. His heat kept the sleeping chambers warm, and he
would not let anyone take the sleepers away. Odile asked for two barrels of water for the refuge;
he allowed one. When Fern offered to carry the sleepers out, he refused: open the channel and the
heat would go with them.

So Odile and Fern ran a trial through an old drain outside his channel, with the chambers shut. It
worked. The chambers stayed dry and warm. Irdrath could see that, and he still would not yield. "I
keep them warm through the night. Who does that on your road?" When the carriers brought the first
stretcher to the tested channel, he ordered the bed put back. "They stay beside my fire."

"They've stayed long enough," Odile said. "Take the head end, please." Irdrath surged toward the
catch, and Fern stepped between him and the stretcher. "Let them through." He said, "Move." They
fought.

Afterward Irdrath gathered thinly around the buried Firstcoal and did not try to stop them again.
He still fretted over the sleepers: "The draught. Keep it off their feet." He held the door clear
while they passed. "Chamber nine next." Odile already had chamber nine on her list.

### 7. Ketter and the duel

By a stopped tram in the Rootways, Fern was studying the archive plan when Vesper came up and read
over Fern's shoulder. "That's a bell tower. The road runs underneath." Fern laid the old ledger's
record beside the plan. Vesper read to the bottom, then back to the first name.

"The same promise," Fern said. "Both times."

"And nothing here about how either bearer died."

Fern offered the rest of the ledger. Vesper's hand moved toward the pack, then stopped. "Keep it
dry. I'll want the rest." Then they left by the lower road.

In the Glass Warrens, a voice echoed down three passages at once. Only one of them had a lamp that
moved, and a living person under it: Ketter, stranded below a broken landing. "Don't pull that
rope — it's frayed through!" Fern secured the lift and brought them up. Ketter kept the bells and
lamps working, and showed Fern which relay handle carried the voice: "Upper branch carries the
voice. Lower one feeds our lamps."

Vesper was waiting there, hand on the upper handle. "You took your time." They wanted it left open
so they could follow the voice. Fern suggested closing it and reading the rest of the ledger
together. Vesper's fingers lifted from the handle, and for a moment neither of them moved.

"You want me to go back with you. Carry a lamp. Wait for orders."

"I want you away from that handle."

Fern stepped forward. Vesper drew their weapon. "Stop there."

Fern won the duel. Vesper fell back toward the far passage, breathing hard, and when Fern called
"Vesper. Wait," a lamp on the lowest landing flickered. Fern saved its feed, and by the time Fern
turned around, Vesper had gone. The upper branch was closed and Ketter's lights stayed on, but the
central relay farther below was still running.

### 8. The bells and the Lishal

In the Bell Hollows, Ketter's supper had gone cold with a skin across the bowl. Bells kept calling
them away from it: another lift had stuck between landings. Fern helped mend the circuit. When
Ketter began to explain the signals one more time, the relief worker answered first: "I know.
You've told me every shift this week." Ketter's hand stopped short of the cord. "Right. You have
it."

Ketter handed over a drawer of plans and letters that Fennick had sent. One letter mapped the
surface ward stations, with a line marked "lower return" that ended in a blank space. Someone had
to go down and look.

At the Quiet Foundry stood the Lishal: seven singers around a great sounding frame with seven
crystal sockets. Their care was real. One bandaged a burned hand, one brought food, one rolled out
bedding. But when a worker said, "You told us about the sleep. I heard you. I don't want it," the
singers began to close the doors. One turned a catch, another pulled bedding across the passage, a
third blocked the other exit. Then all seven sang as one: "Close the outer doors. Bring the beds
through."

Ketter wedged the loading door with their own repair box. A falling shutter struck a worker's leg,
and Ketter left the tools behind to help the worker out. "Leave it. Arm round my shoulder." On the
inspection gallery beyond the frame, Fern found the missing piece of Fennick's drawing and copied
it.

### 9. Vesper's choice

Across the turning frame, Vesper stood on a far platform. "Listen to it. Seven voices, and they can
barely carry a whisper." The voice spoke from the stone: _"You need not wait for them."_

"I have waited quite enough."

Light spread beneath Vesper's feet as the voice offered itself: _"Take my voice with you. The
stones will answer you wherever you go."_ Fern lowered a footbridge to Vesper, but just then a
warning bell rang below. The loading shutter was falling across the workers' exit, and Fern caught
its brake and held on.

"You found a way across," Vesper said.

"Come over."

Vesper took one step toward the bridge. Fern could see the cut from their duel. "And let you shut
all this away?" Fern reminded them of the other bearers in the ledger. "I read every one." The frame
swung between them. Vesper's fingers closed on the rail. When it passed again, they had let go.

"No. I'm staying. Let me hear it. All of it."

Light climbed their arms, and the bells rang with their next breath. They staggered once, then
straightened. "I can hear the surface." Then they left by the service ascent. Fern kept hold of the
brake until Ketter called that the last worker was clear.

Fern disconnected the branch feeding the frame, and the seven colours faded. The Lishal would not
accept it. "They won't stay out. They'll tire. They'll be hurt." Their rooms were empty, but there
were others above. They fitted a handle into the manual drive and took their places. "Begin
again." Fern fought them and stilled the frame, and the little lift bell still worked. But Vesper
had already taken the voice beyond the relay and up toward Sevenstones.

### 10. The return and the rite

A fallen beam blocked the upward road. Fern turned the jack wheel while Ketter worked underneath and
Odile kept the waiting sleepers back. A relief operator below started the lift exactly right, and
Ketter stopped before giving another instruction. "Good. They've got it." A courier came down the
stairs: Fennick had sent word that the violet stone was going dark again. Odile took a bearer's
place and sent Fern on.

At Sevenstones, Fennick had spread the recovered pages over a workbench. He asked one thing after
another. Did Fern see Vesper? Was the way out clear? Could Vesper see it? "They looked at it. Then
they went back to the voice." And was Fern close enough to hear them? "Yes." He kept his finger on the
drawing of the steps for a long time. Then: "Find Vale. We can't do this with two people."

The rite needed a whole community. Fern went to Brightwater first and saw Hareguy, still asleep,
with a volunteer from the watch at his bedside. Back in Mossvale, Rowan put down his rasp before
Fern had crossed the yard. "My boy?" "Still asleep." He asked whether Pete had been charging for the
room and sent coin from the jar under the nails. He wouldn't come to Sevenstones ("I'd slow you
down"), but he corrected the wedges for the station frames before anyone had cut them wrong. Bean
counted out three bundles of herbs, hesitated over a fourth and kept it. Ordor loaned his handcart
and lamp oil, and told Fern to bring the cart back with both wheels.

Around the ward, frames went up at every stone. Whichever mentors Fern had befriended came to help
at their own stones. Torva's hands were steady on the red stone ("Must be you, spark"), and Selene
offered, "The violet stone went first, and it will go last. Hold it with me, one more time."

The recovered drawing showed that Fennick's flameless brass lantern belonged in the ring itself. "I've been
putting it beside the ward," he said. In a chalk test, Ketter pressed one frame and it
rocked. "That corner's moved." "I checked it," Fennick said. Then he crouched to help fix it, and
asked Ketter to check every other frame too. Pale light gathered in the wickless lantern. When Fern
stepped out of the circle, the light stayed inside. The records said the voice could not cross the
ring. Nobody yet knew whether that held for a living voice.

Vale asked what would happen if Vesper didn't fall. "Then they're still carrying it," Fennick said.
"We keep the stations manned and give the watch time to get everyone else away." He set the lantern
in its seat on the outer ring, and the inner ward opened.

### 11. The Malificat

Light moved beneath Vesper's skin. Their shape stretched as seven streams of colour drew together
into them. "You should have stayed to watch," they said. "I'm drawing on all seven." That was why
the stones were going dark.

A second voice spoke through them: "The outer ring. Break it." Vesper glanced past Fern, to the
old man at the lantern. "Fennick?" Fennick looked up but did not leave his post. Vesper stepped
toward the entrance, and a loose strand of their light struck the boundary and sprang back. "What
have you done?"

"The voice can't leave this chamber," Fern said.

Vesper looked at the light in their hand and closed it. The Malificat unfolded fully: "You think you
can take it from me." Through all of it they were still recognisably Vesper, with the same
searching tilt of the head.

Fern won. The current between Vesper and the stones broke, and the altered shape collapsed. The
voice struck the boundary and echoed back from every wall. Vesper lay breathing, the light gone. The
voice called their name. Vesper tried to lift a hand, and nothing gathered in it. Fennick started
forward, and Vale put an arm across the entrance: "Wait. Let us check it." The watch carried Vesper
out past the ring. The voice stayed behind. Only when everyone was clear did the stations close the
inner ward.

What the voice actually is, how old it is and how far it controlled the Wyrm, the game never says.
It is simply sealed in.

### 12. Recovery

Outside the ward, Vesper lay on a watch blanket. Fennick reached toward their hand, then folded the
blanket around their shoulder instead. "Don't," Vesper said. He drew back and set a cup within
reach. "There's water when you want it." Vale had a room ready, under guard. No one asked the
people Vesper had harmed to forgive them.

Word came from Brightwater that the remaining sleepers were beginning to wake, and Pete sent a note
that Hareguy had asked for breakfast. In the refuge's new lodging, Odile finally put her own things
on a shelf. At Ketter's workshop, another worker answered a signal correctly, and Ketter went out
without correcting them.

In Mossvale, Rowan sat among unused offcuts with Pete's note in his hands. "He's asked for
breakfast." "Pete says he ate it, too." Rowan tried to stand and his knee caught, so he swore and
waited. There was a cart for Brightwater in the morning. "I'll be ready." He put the note in his
coat, took it out, and read the last line again.

Nearby, Pip had added another tier to his tower. "It's not stuck! I just wanted you to see it
before supper." Fern stayed to look, and the village went on around them.

## Part 2 — A possible version of Vesper and Fennick

In this version, Vesper is no longer simply someone who went looking for glory. At heart they want
**to be heard**. As a child they were quiet, one of twelve children in Fennick's care, and they
never got their turn. Fennick, for his part, looked after all twelve, but when it mattered he chose
the ward over listening to Vesper. The main events stay the same. These are the scenes where the
player would notice the difference.

### On the road

Vesper would meet Fern as a rival rather than a sneering servant of a "Master". They would race
Fern through the same puzzles, keep count of Fern's moves, and want to be noticed: "Nobody on this
road has asked my name all week. You didn't either. But you looked." At the quarry they say Torva
sent them away. Beneath the ridge, something hums at night, and "it says my name right." At the
Moonfall ruins, with a palm flat on the cave seal, Vesper says the line the whole version turns on:
"I asked Selene about the moon. She said wait. I've waited. **It's my turn.**" Any player on decent
terms with Vesper would hear it before the Wyrm.

### At the stair

Fennick's first introduction would gain four words: "I looked after them when they were small. Them
and eleven others." It is a small detail, but the player would remember it later.

### Taking turns

The Rootways scene by the tram would become a quiet one. Fern puts the ledger down, and Vesper
won't take it. "Read it out. I like hearing it." So they take turns, one name each, down the page.
Fern passes on Fennick's message, and Vesper answers: "He was always there. At the ward. He read to
twelve of us at once. You had to be loud to get a page turned." Fern asks whether they were loud.
"No. Were you? Back home?" Fern admits nearly staying behind to watch the sleeping houses. Vesper
looks at Fern a moment longer than they need to.

### The duel

One line in the duel would change. Vesper's "Carry a lamp. Wait for orders" would become "Stand in
line. **Wait my turn again.**" With the childhood behind it, the duel stops being about pride. It is
about Vesper refusing, one more time, to be told to wait.

### The bridge

The bridge stays where it is, and Vesper still lets go of the rail. The difference is what the
player now understands as it happens. When the voice says, "You need not wait for them," it is
offering exactly what Vesper has wanted since childhood. "Seven voices, and they can barely carry a
whisper" now reads as contempt for the very thing Vesper never had: being one voice among many and
still being heard. Vesper turns Fern's own hesitation from the tram back on them ("You didn't want
to leave your houses with nobody watching. I won't leave this"), and Fern answers with both hands on
the brake. After "I can hear the surface," Vesper adds one more line: "And they'll all hear me."
The choice is just as free and just as wrong. It is easier to understand and harder to watch.

### Fennick's admission

Back at the workbench, Fennick would ask the question he is afraid of: "What did they say?" Fern
tells him. Then he remembers: "When they were small they sat in the top window. They said they could
hear the town from there. I told them to come down and eat with the others." A pause. "I should
have gone up." Fern tells him gently that the choice on that platform was Vesper's own. Fennick's
last word in the scene becomes a plan: "If they call for me in there, I'll answer from the lantern."

The rite also gains weight. When Fennick accepts Ketter's correction about the loose frame, it is
the first time we see him truly listen to one person.

### The Malificat

Vesper's boast would become "I'm drawing on all seven. Can you hear how loud I am?" When they call
"Fennick?", he answers this time: "I'm here." He does not leave the lantern. For a breath the light
in Vesper's hand goes still. Then the voice twists the truth to wound them: "He stayed outside. He
always does. Break it." After the fight, Vesper says only, "It's gone quiet," and Fern kneels and
carries them out of the ward.

### Recovery

Vesper is still alive, still under guard, and still not forgiven by anyone on anyone else's behalf.
Fern sets the dry ledger beside the cup: "You wanted the rest." What Vesper does next depends on
how the friendship has gone. A cold Vesper leaves both untouched. A warmer one asks, "Was it dry?"
At the warmest, Vesper drinks, opens the ledger and reads the first name aloud. Fennick says
nothing. He listens.
