## 1. The company and POOF

### The Realm **[P]**

**[P] The Realm is the whole world.** It has uninhabited continents; a great
cataclysm left everyone living on just one. The cataclysm is also where magic
is believed to have dropped out of the zeitgeist, and magic came to be thought
of as myth and legend. The Dungeon's discovery changed all of that. The one
inhabited continent is divided into several commonwealths — most likely; not
yet firm.

**[P] Places, largest to smallest:** commonwealth · fiefdom · barony · town ·
village · fort. A **castle** is not a level; it can stand within a
commonwealth, fiefdom, barony or town. An address names a place at the scale
the customer knows.

### The rediscovery **[P]**

**A generation ago, 'The Dungeon' was found.** Soon after, the Adventurer's
Guild was founded. The first party went in search of treasure; those brave
souls were never found, though later their gear was discovered. During the
official investigation an ancient scroll was found sewn between the layers of
a bedroll. Read aloud, it sent up a geyser of flame that destroyed the
remaining evidence. Several guilds were formed the very next day, including
the Magicians' Guild.

**[P] The original Magician.** The person who read the scroll — later the head
of TLC's R&D — founded the
**Magician's** Guild and began researching and studying: hard labour in the
catacombs of knowledge — the nearby university library — and a few near misses
journeying with adventurers in search of further scrolls and other ancient
knowledge, some of which was still legible. That it often wasn't is not only
down to age. In the **first couple of years at most**, the overuse of what has
become known as **Fireball** was a blessing, since it removed a great many
obstacles, and a curse, because when your only tool is a hammer every problem
is a nail — and fireballs burn things, while knowledge tends to be recorded on
flammable materials. **[P]** Those early misadventures with damaged or partial
scrolls came from the Magician's own lack of knowledge, and faded as skill grew
through practice and experimentation. The Magician got better at Fireball.

**[P]** Magical knowledge expanded, and as the adventurers became more
competent and skilled, so did the magicians. When it moved from a
**Magician's** Guild to the more respectable-sounding **Magicians'** Guild, it
was able to join the co-op. That they had by then discovered a way to create a
refillable potion was a happy accident and complete coincidence.

**[P]** When POOF was discovered, establishing an R&D team internally made
sense — and who better to head it than the original Magician, who would never
pass up the chance to learn more.

**[C·ok] The Magician was a new agent with one card.** A short window at the
very start, when the only person who could read the scrolls was the least able
to handle them, and nobody else to ask. Whatever burned in those two years
burned because nobody yet knew enough not to burn it. **[C]** It could earn its
keep in the game: a coworker telling the story that way to an apprentice whose
call has just gone wrong with the only question they knew to ask.

**[C·ok]** So TLC didn't employ the founder at the start. The guild joined the
co-op once it was a guild, and R&D came later, with POOF.

**[P] Every enchantment in the world came from the guild.** There is no
unsanctioned magic. **Fireball is a cautionary tale for the general public
too: magic can be unpredictable, the Magicians' Guild is not.**

**[C]** So the guild's standing rests on being the safe and repeatable option,
and the story of the scroll does the enforcing socially — nobody sensible reads
a strange scroll aloud. It also keeps the rules whole: every enchanted item has
a provenance, and the gaps an agent runs into come from records being imperfect
rather than from anything outside the rules. **[P] Rejected:** magic practised
outside the guild — an escape hatch that would undo the settled rules.

**[P]** *The Dungeon* is only the first one found. Because of the tragedy and
the discovery, the name has taken on a life of its own.

**[P] Timeline:** about two decades in fact; *"a generation ago"* is how people
say it, and the phrase carries the gravitas. **[C]** Twenty years keeps the
first practitioners still in post, still arguing, still wrong about things, and
the institutions young enough to be improvised — which is why R&D has basics
and not a manual, and why a co-op that moved parcels for a century runs magic
out of a new division. At thirty-five there'd be a second generation who grew
up with it, and they would have standardised things. It also dates Sella's
flask: early-model equipment, sold to someone told how to use it and not how
it works.

**[C] What the premise pays for:** R&D needs no justification · the knowledge
gaps are the world rather than a design choice, so customer confusion isn't
stupidity — everyone is a novice, including the people selling it · logistics
came first and magic was bolted onto a company that already moved goods, so
the tier split is a historical accident rather than a pricing decision ·
adventurers recover things nobody understands, which is why a shop shelf can
be full of items whose uses haven't been worked out.

**[C]** Magic came back by accident and its first act destroyed the evidence:
honest error, no villain, information lost at the moment it was needed. Spoken
words having consequences is the oldest fact in this world.

**[P] Wordplay** works when it's a parallel from our world that a character
would say plainly — *enchanted mail* — not a wink at the audience. The joke
happens on our side of the page.

### The co-op and the guilds **[P]**

**[P]** The name is the **Transportation & Logistical Co-operative (TLC)**.

**[P] Guilds existed long before the Dungeon** — Farmers' Guilds, various
[Shop]keeper Guilds, Innkeeper Guilds. Would they not need a little TLC too?

**[C·ok]** So **TLC predates the rediscovery**: a carrier built by working
guilds who needed goods moved, already there when the new guilds turned up.
*Serving every guild* is the founding arrangement, not a slogan. The
Adventurer's and Magicians' Guilds joined something that existed, which is why
the Magicians' Guild being part of the co-op with no rival is ordinary rather
than suspicious. **[C]** Most calls are mundane — a farmer's delivery, an
innkeeper's order, a shopkeeper's stock — and POOF is the loud minority, which
is the right balance for a cozy game. It also explains the Council: farmers and
innkeepers voting on an experimental magic service, hence terms, a buyback and
a research budget rather than free rein. **[C]** My earlier *"moved parcels for
a century"* was invented; the co-op is old, the number isn't set.

**[C·ok] The next-day guilds** were mostly splits from trades that already
existed: the **Armorers'** out of the smiths; the **Apothecaries'** out of the
herbalists and brewers, since adventurers came back injured and a draught you
can carry is worth more than a poultice at home; and the **Explorers'** out of
the cartographers and scribes. **[P]** The Explorers travel with the
Adventurers, the way a bard would — the Adventurer seeks the excitement and
mystery of the unknown, the Explorer seeks to map and record it. The
**Magicians'** was founded by the Magician alone.

**[P] Partners** are individual guild-affiliated shops and traveling merchants
alike. The shops are run by senior journeymen or masters, and they are what an
apprentice works toward (§9).

**[P] Partner is a relationship**, like the customer relationships in §18.
TLC moves goods through traveling merchants who hold that relationship with
it. There are different classes of traveling merchant; they bring goods to
people with no guild nearby, carry stock between guilds, and resell. They know
their local areas, and hire adventurers for protection when they go somewhere
less familiar. Villains and anti-heroes normally leave them alone: hitting one
angers the Guilds, and almost everyone has a subscription.

**[P] Healing draughts** are made and sold by apothecaries and magicians, and
sold by merchants, shops, inns and other logical vendors.

**[P] Departments a call can be transferred to:** Partner Support · Billing ·
Subscriptions · Sales · Repairs · Dispatch. R&D and account administration
don't take calls.

### Commerce **[P]**

**[P] The tiering was for the end customers**, not for guild membership. Not
every brand-new adventurer has the money or the skills to afford the best
product or service straight away, and the guilds still want to serve their own
customers, so they wanted choices and options to offer them.

**[C·ok]** So the pay-wall exists so the poorest customer can be served at all,
not to keep them out. The cheap tier is the beginner's tier — which puts the
least experienced agents and the least resourced customers in the same
conversation. Entitlements follow the tier, which is what makes Sella's
complimentary refill a find rather than a gift: it depends on how many her
tier allows and how many she has already used this term. Someone who has used
theirs gets half off instead, and that is where the better No lives. The agent
has to know the tier before speaking — the three-part check again.

**[C·ok] The tiers are Copper, Silver and Gold.** **[P]** Complimentary refills per annual
subscription term: one on Copper, three on Silver, five on Gold. Beyond those,
refills are half off.

**[P] Currency** is copper, silver and gold coin: 10 coppers to a silver, 5
silvers to a gold.

**[P]** Healing draughts come in strengths, as potions do in most RPGs.
**[C·ok]** In plain words — no numbers shown:
- **Minor** — everyday knocks: cuts, bruises, a turned ankle. Gets you home,
  not back into the fight. 2 coppers at a shop.
- **Standard** — a proper wound: a sword cut, a bad fall. Back on your feet
  the same day. 1 silver.
- **Greater** — serious harm: a broken bone, a deep wound. The one that saves
  a life. 1 gold.
- **Superior** — close to death; carried into a dungeon and hoped never to be
  opened. 2 gold, shop only — on no plan.

**[C·ok] The tier sets the strength the flask refills with:** Copper refills
minor, Silver standard, Gold greater. Per annual term, payable monthly: Copper
5 gold (about 2 silver a month — less than a new adventurer's first job pays),
Silver 20 gold, Gold 60 gold. Every plan costs far less than a draught a night
from a shop, because most nights it goes undrunk; the co-op prices for it being
there. A refill beyond the allowance is half the shop price for that strength.
**[C]** A customer who drops a tier wakes to a weaker draught, and one who
expects a minor draught to mend a broken arm rings to say the flask doesn't
work.

**[C·ok] Customers move between tiers over time.** An adventurer who survives a
few years buys up; a shop with a bad season drops down. The record keeps that
history, so an agent can see someone who used to have more than they have now,
which is a different conversation from someone who never had it — and a
customer who remembers what they used to be entitled to will argue from the old
tier.

**[C] Offered:** provenance claims. Anything recovered is worth more if it came
from The Dungeon, so people say it did. The co-op can't verify it and has no
reason to argue, so a record and a customer's story can carry two different
provenances for one object.

### POOF **[P]**

**[P]** TLC is the company and the standard service. **POOF** is the new,
experimental, express service.

### POOF Instant **[P]**

**[P] POOF Instant** is a delivery line, not one product: **Potions ·
Weapons · Armour · Accessories**. Fantasy e-commerce, run by the co-op the
guilds built to handle it.

**[P] Nothing is refilled.** The process: ingredients are loaded into an
appropriately sized flask, per the customer's request · the shipment is sent ·
the customer receives a finished potion · **empty flasks may be returned for a
refund of the flask deposit fee.** The flask is TLC's, on deposit; nothing of
the customer's travels.

**[C·ok]** So "refill" is the customer's word for it, and the wrong one — which
is a correction worth making to her, and it stays on her sheet once made.

**[P] Five ways an order can be made up:**
1. **Standard** — predetermined ingredients, a consistent R&D recipe.
2. **Standard on subscription** — the same recipe, on a standing arrangement.
3. **Custom from TLC stock** — the customer chooses ingredients and pays for
   higher quality, or for adding to the recipe.
4. **Custom from the customer's bag** — made from what they have on hand in
   the shared inventory.
5. **Custom from both** — their bag plus TLC stock for the gaps.

**[C·ok]** Options 4 and 5 need a bag with something in it. A villager who
buys twice a year has none, so offering it spends a play and returns nothing.

**[P] Supporting a POOF Instant that went wrong** — was the recipe followed ·
what ingredients were in it · were there additional inclusions, and what ·
what was the shipping container · was it custom or standard · what was the
custom one made from. **[C]** And: what condition the ingredients were in ·
whether the quantities matched the recipe · whether anything was in the
container that was not part of the order · whose ingredients came out of the
bag · how many went in one send · and who assembled it.

### POOF Instant request types **[P]**

**[P]** A request is **POOF Instant + the request type** — *POOF Instant
Repair* is the official title of that one. **Quote** is a modifier rather
than a sibling: a POOF Instant Quote is a quote *for* a request type, not a
request of its own.

**[P] Order** — customers requesting POOF shipments for their businesses:
**Potions · Weapons · Armour · Accessories**.

**[P] Repair** — the same list **minus Potions**, which are consumable rather
than reusable. **Artifacts** are applicable, but a rare occurrence, and
likely need very specific ingredients.

**[P] Ingredients** — a **Guild** can order ingredients as a standalone
order. Each item comes as its own container of ingredients, to avoid a POOF
interaction.

**[P] Deliveries** are the end result of a shipment, either to the recipient
or to TLC. **[P] Customer Service** is the went-wrong category.

**[P] Containers.** A flask is a container for a potion. A container for
multiple flasks is needed. Otherwise a container is standard or custom based
on the customer's choice.

**[P] Flask and container returns** are done via a local Guild member, like a
glass bottled pop used to be at stores, and **the Guild hands over the
deposit refund**.

**[C] Enhancement** — ingredients mixed with an item the customer already
owns, for an enhancement rather than a net new item. It cannot be an Order,
since nothing of the customer's travels on one, and it is not a Repair, since
nothing is broken.

**[C] Outbound against round trip.** Order, Ingredients and Quote are
outbound only. Repair and Enhancement are **round trips** — the customer's
own property travels. That is where the risk sits: a failed Order costs a
shipment, a failed Enhancement costs them the item, and because existing
upgrades **are** the item's nature, a mismatched send can step it somewhere
they did not want and cannot undo.

**[C]** So Enhancement carries a support question the others do not: what was
**already** on the item, because that is half the reaction.

**[C]** The standalone ingredient order is the first thing in the design that
uses a POOF rule by **preventing** it — multiples share a roll, so separate
containers are what stop stock mixing in transit.

**[C]** The deposit being handed over at the counter means the customer is
made whole on the spot and never chases money. **[C] Withdrawn, mine:** a
category of calls disputing the handover; it made a mess of the rest.

**[P·C] The ordinary call is arranging a POOF Instant** and dealing with
unexpected results, before battles. **Battles are the loud minority.**
**[C] Superseded:** my earlier inference that parcels are the ordinary call
and POOF the loud minority.

**[P]** POOF is a spell. It does not ride ley lines. The Magicians' Guild
provides it, and the Magicians' Guild is part of the co-op. There is no rival.

**[P]** Goods sent by POOF may end up with unintended buffs, sometimes
debuffs, occasionally both. That is part of the allure — buy something, ship
it to yourself at your destination, and gamble that it improves.

**[P]** The end result is tracked — it's an experiment, so results are
recorded. The result is part of the tracking information, available to the
player with the appropriate customer information.

**[P]** POOF's terms: experimental, so outcomes are not guaranteed; the good
outweighs the bad; an outcome is **not reversible**; there are two choices —
a new item delivered via POOF, or TLC purchases (and resells) the newly
enchanted item.

**[C·ok]** The locked tagline still works, since TLC and POOF are the same
company's two tiers: *"When your package needs more than TLC, pick POOF.
It's there before you blink."*

**[C]** A POOF complaint is TLC's to handle, not a referral.

### Rescue by POOF

**[P]** Adventurers on quests call for help and use POOF to get resupplied;
the player can also use POOF to try to save them. A portion of what
adventurers earn is tithed to TLC as stock for those deliveries. The chaos
comes when items don't arrive as expected, and the player puzzles out what
went wrong through the conversation. **[C·ok]** Rescue calls are the loud
minority among mundane ones.

**[P] Failure is a lost quest**, not a death. It is written to the party's
character sheets and changes which items and components are available next
time.

**[P] The bag that holds anything** is TLC holding the adventurer's goods, with
POOF fetching them. **[C·ok]** A party shares one bag, under the CRM's Admin
and User access, so the member who calls may not see what another member put
in or took out.

**[P]** POOF's rules are fixed, like a recipe, yet people make it better or
worse, add to it, modify it, and manipulate the rules for a different
outcome — and the quality of the ingredients matters too. Knowing the rule
tells the player what should have happened; the call is finding what this
party did differently.

**[P]** Ley lines and the size upcharge belong to the previous iteration.
`Quest Line Bug`, `Respawn Delay` and `XP Miscount Dispute` belong to a
support center for video game characters. Neither is this game.

### What R&D is asking **[P]**

Raised 20 September while working Sella's call. My earlier guess — effects
that rhyme or bleed between items sent together — is withdrawn in favour of
these.

**A single item.** A sword is sent via POOF: are all swords affected equally?
Does material matter to the outcome? Construction? Quality? Are existing
upgrades impacted? Are existing upgrades taken into account when effects are
applied?

**In a container.** Does the composition of the container matter? Its
quality? Its materials? Does it change POOF's cost? Does it change the
outcome? Do existing upgrades affect anything? Are they taken into account
when effects are applied?

**Multiples.** Same item, same enhancements? Same item, different
enhancements? Do composition, quality or materials matter? Existing
upgrades? Potential upgrades?

**The maker.** Does the skill of the craftsperson who made it affect the
item they created?

**Unmade things.** If the ingredients for a potion are put in a bottle and
sent, could the result be a random potion of that class, without upgrades?
What if the cloth, pattern and thread for a cloak were sent — could the
result be a random cloak, without upgrades?

**[P] What R&D has.** The basics are nailed down — like the basic recipes in
most crafting games, the minimum needed to get your result. Beyond that, the
agent has wiggle room: **R&D-approved enhancements** — safe to add, and R&D
wants more data on what happens when they are — which the agent can offer as
additional inserts to shape the end result.

**[P]** An insert becomes an option for the player, from the basics. **A
beginner's basics are different from an experienced agent's.** **[C]** By
§9, a beginner may still offer something beyond their basics — improvement
grants reliability, never permission — it simply lands less predictably.

**[P]** R&D is still a driving factor: it has to work with **item
availability** for the Available Add-In Options. **[C]** Two things move,
then — what the agent knows, and what is on the shelf today. Availability is
what stops a player settling on one small set of options that works, finding
the pattern, winning, and finding the game boring.

**[P]** Checking availability before offering takes three things: **knowing**
to check, **remembering** to check, and **doing it properly**. A new agent
ranges anywhere from none of those to all three, and which ones show up can
change from call to call. An experienced agent usually has all three, and
can still drop one.

### POOF's basic rules — in progress **[P]**

**[P]** When left open to interpretation, PEOPLE will find a way. Each rule
is decided knowing what guilds will do with it. Admins and Users are
fundamentally different experiences — an Admin plus any permission is a
different experience from a User plus the same permission — so the variables
multiply rather than add.

**[P] Equations** *(enhancement simplified out):*
- Magic + Item = Enhanced Item
- Magic + Broken/Damaged Item + Raw Material = Repaired Item
- Magic + Raw Material recipe = Random Quality Item
- Magic + Item A + Item B = Shared Enhancement, **or** do A and B shape
  each other's outcome? *(e.g. Sword + Fire Scroll = Fire Sword & Piercing
  Fire Scroll — negative effect left out)* — **under discussion**
- Magic + (Quantity) of Item = Shared Enhancement, with layers where the
  quality of each item shapes the quality of its enhancement

**[C] Multiples.** The batch shares one roll — same kind of enhancement —
and each item's own quality sets how strongly it takes. Scouting with one
item first tells you nothing; the next send is a fresh roll.

**[P] Minor damage.** A chipped sword sent alone reforms properly, perhaps
slightly smaller — it uses its own substance. Most chips are on the cutting
edge, which a sharpening fixes without POOF at all.

**[P] Material matters, and POOF reforges rather than patches.** A damaged
mythril sword sent with iron comes back as one alloy blade — no seam — with
the strengths and weaknesses of both. **[C]** This keeps POOF from turning
iron into mythril. The proportions decide the blend.

**[P] Components.** Components sent together make the item, at a random
quality. **[C]** The ingredients set the range and the roll picks inside it,
so cheap components and a lucky roll can't beat honest sourcing. A quantity
of sets gives that many items sharing one roll, each taking it by its own
share: matched ingredients come out close, never identical; mixed ones
spread, and a good set doesn't lift the poor ones. Consistent sourcing is a
guild skill.

**[P]** When a guild rings about an uneven batch, the solution includes
**sharing knowledge**, which reduces customer effort in the long run.

**[P]** *"I sent extra to be safe, though…"* — a customer who oversupplied
the cheaper material gets a diluted item. **Guilds learning the right amount
is a skill.**

**[P] Different items together.** Phil's proposal, now worked through
aspect by aspect below: the items share the enhancement. A regular sword sent with
a flame scroll gets a flame-adjacent enhancement by default; two flame-based
items would likely trigger a flame-based upgrade instead. The sword's roll
draws on sword-based enhancements (piercing, keen edge, bleed, and others);
the scroll's enhancement is adjusted by the sword's basic nature, picking up
a sword-based enhancement. So the enhancement allows magical items to be
created, and magical items to gain perks beyond a standard scroll.

**[C·ok] Priority** goes to the **stronger nature**, not to magic as such.
Magical items usually carry the stronger nature, so they usually lead; a
masterwork mundane item can outweigh a minor magical one.

**[C·ok] Adjacent.** The partner's nature arrives somewhere between
**adjacent** — what it does or leaves behind (for fire: heat, light, smoke,
ash, sparks, drying, cauterising) — and **full**. How strong that nature is
decides how far along the range it can reach. **[P]** Shipping an item with
an ingredient gives some cross-pollination, at a lesser level than a quality
item with a shared nature could achieve. Remnants of an **ingredient** are
viable; remnants of a **scroll** are a damaged item, subject to repair POOF,
not enhancement.

**[P·C] Matching natures step the tier up** — like Final Fantasy's Blizzard,
Blizzara, Blizzaga. **[C·ok]** Strength decides how many steps a result can
climb. There is no fixed top tier: **each step gets harder to reach**, until
only the strongest pairings can make it.

**[P] Receive equally** — *"the enhancement is framed by whichever leads, and
fleshed out by the other(s). I think…"* **[C]** One shared enhancement, the
same on every item, each wearing it through its own nature: a strong flame
scroll leading a plain sword gives *piercing flame* on both.

**[P·C] Struck:** the proposed rule that what a follower adds must make the
leader better at what it already does. There is no such rule — there is a
**spectrum**. At one end, a clear improvement to what the leader does (an
edge that burns as it cuts). At the other, a **shelf item**: technically an
enhancement, useful only when circumstances line up — a blade that keeps a pot
warm is precisely that. Everything in between, and nothing falls off either
end, since POOF never makes the item worse and never guarantees something
excellent. Where a result lands is decided by the same things as everything
else: how strong the natures are, how well they match, the quality of what
went in, and the roll.

**[P] Fire upgrades for a sword** — tempered · burning · flaming · melting ·
cauterising · others. **[C·ok] Kept:** **searing** — a sharp burn at contact,
then gone; near burning, clearly an improvement to the cut · **smouldering** —
the blade glows faintly, no flame; light without a torch, and it shows where
you are · **warding** — never frosts, sticks in the scabbard or rusts in the
wet; toward the shelf end, no help in a fight and quietly excellent for anyone
living somewhere cold and wet.

**[P] Burn** is the common video-game equivalent of bleed — an ongoing,
draining after-effect. **[P]** It is not an upgrade on the list; some
upgrades qualify for the burn effect because of the nature of the upgrade.

**[C·ok] Range and burn do different jobs.** The range decides which upgrades
can be reached — flaming *is* fire, so it needs full strength; tempered and
warding are what fire does to steel, reachable from the adjacent end.
Whether an upgrade leaves burn behind comes from what it is, not where it
sat. A customer who sent a weak fire ingredient cannot get a flaming blade.

**[P·C] Opposed natures are their own mechanism** — safer, and it fits the
lore. Matching natures step up a tier; opposed natures produce the odd,
situational result instead.

**[P] Rejected:** elemental arithmetic — steam, scalding, freeze-drying.
*"They feel stupid."* **[P] Rejected:** any result that leaves the item
functionally **less** than it was — POOF doesn't downgrade, so brittle steel
is out. The cost is awkwardness, not loss of function, as with Ivo's heavier
cloak.

**[P] What the results are for.** They are the reason shops in games and
media carry randomly, sort-of-useful weapons, armour and items — things for
a class that makes no sense until later, or under specific circumstances.
**[C]** POOF's buyback term is the supply chain for that shelf: someone
gambled, didn't want the result, sold it back. So a result doesn't need to
be individually clever; it needs to be the **kind of thing that ends up on
that shelf** — specific, situational, and pointless until it isn't.

**[P] Struck:** the steam-wreathed blade (arithmetic) and the item made
indifferent to what it was paired against (a **recipe** — pair with ice to
become ice-proof, every time, which turns POOF into a crafting bench).
**[C]** Both came from asking what two elements make. The results that work
come from what an item is for and who owns it.

**[P·C] Six worked examples** — a flaming sword sent with something cold:

1. **It burns where fire can't** — underwater, in an airless tunnel, in a
   sealed vault. The only light and the only working weapon below ground or
   below water; an ordinary flaming sword everywhere else.
2. **It takes fire into itself.** Flame it touches goes out and goes in, and
   the blade carries it until spent. Walk it through a burning barn and you've
   put the barn out and armed yourself with it.
3. **Its burns deepen in cold.** A wound from it doesn't settle; the colder
   the air, the worse it gets. Unremarkable in a warm room, brutal in a
   northern winter.
4. **Its flame never goes out.** You can't sheathe it, set it down indoors,
   carry it through a stable or sleep beside it — and it is an endless source
   of fire, worth a great deal to anyone needing fire constantly and reliably:
   a forge, a kiln, a bakehouse, a beacon.
5. **What it cuts keeps burning until it's put in water.** Time doesn't end
   it. Water does, and nothing else.
6. **Whoever carries it is never cold.** Not the blade, the owner. A night
   without a fire, a swim through meltwater, a season above the snowline.
   Worth a life in the north and nothing at all in summer.

**[P] Rejected along the way:** *cuts frozen matter like cloth* (dumb) ·
*wounds that cold healing can't close* (reworked into 3) · *fiercer flame in
colder air* (too close to 3) · *burns nothing it isn't swung at*, *whatever it
cuts keeps*, *held safely by the blade*, *the blade sweats water*, *the
scabbard keeps its contents' temperature*, *a flame that gives no light* — all
household trivia rather than situational power, with two of them the same
preservation idea twice.

**[P] Containers.** A container could be inlaid with magical gems, mystical
metals, sorcerer's-staff wood — high-value items repurposed to influence the
contents without paying shipping on them within the POOF payment. *"Can I
help it that the container is worth more as parts than as a container? It
was the scrap I had, and I needed these items contained for the POOF bulk
rate."*

**[C] Rejected:** that POOF treats the container as just more of the send,
enhanced along with the contents. **[P]** It is not the same as sending a
sword with the scraps you had left over — you'd hoped to have fresh stock,
but this shipment must now go POOF.

**[P] The container influences the magic.** The magic penetrates the
container and influences what's inside. Where it passes through an enchanted
part of the container, what reaches the items differs from what a
**standard, official TLC container** would let through — and that one is
likely boring, traditional, cheap, run-of-the-mill quality. *(Illustrated by
lead affecting an x-ray. Lead works against the x-ray; inlays work for the
magic. The point is the influence, not the lead.)*

**[C·ok] The inlays wear.** The container isn't enhanced; it gives. Each send
draws a little of the inlays' nature, until it's a well-made plain box.
Without wear, one good crate is a permanent free boost. **[P]** The crate is
**not TLC's product.** And because results are random and depend on the
contents, a customer can't readily see the wear in their results.

**[C] Withdrawn:** that worn inlays produce a "POOF's got worse" call. It
fails the §15 test — the complaint doesn't survive being asked about, since
POOF's outcomes were never guaranteed and nothing failed.

**[P]** Shippers don't want to draw attention to a workaround they crafted to
improve their POOF outcomes. Any good business would seek to close that
loophole, as it could appear as lost revenue.

**[P] What does bring contact:** a damaged container, POOF creating a hole in
the container, an inlay disappearing. **[C·ok]** These survive the §15 test —
something physical happened to the customer's property. The customer has a
real complaint and a reason to understate it: *"the crate came back
damaged,"* not *"the inlay I used to improve my results has vanished."*

**[P]** It opens an opportunity for someone from R&D to speak with the
player. The player has limited knowledge of how POOF works; R&D has more.
**[C·ok]** R&D gets field evidence it can't produce itself; the player gets
knowledge that enters their basics and stays. What the player then shares
with customers is filtered — what this customer can use, what policy allows,
and where it sits in the sentence. Customers keep it, tell their guilds,
behaviour spreads, and it comes back as more calls and more data.

**[C·ok] Billing and R&D.** At first billing is oblivious — the line is on
every order and nobody reads it. Once billing starts to notice, R&D
interferes, because the data is too valuable. Nobody is the villain.

**[C·ok] Keeping the tension cozy:** no villain; no punishment for which way
the player leans — it costs goodwill or a favour, never their job or
standing; and **the player is never required to lie to a customer.** The
tension is over what to share, not what to say falsely.

**[P] Determining the damage.** The player needs to determine the damage;
what happens next depends on what they find. Damage where the inlay would
be is only a problem if it came **from outside** and pierced it. A hole
where **POOF removed material** is a different animal — it leaves a distinct
gap. Instead of influencing, metal from a sword inlay was used to **repair**,
and so disappeared.

**[P] How it happens:** two shipments going at once — one for repair, one for
enhancement via POOF, of the same items. Someone makes a simple mistake, a
broken item goes in the wrong crate, and POOF — the container is "damaged."
**[P]** Or they knowingly sent a broken sword. **[C]** Either way, both
orders sit on the account, so the clue is findable by an agent who thinks to
look.

**[P] How POOF's quirk was discovered — lore.** An adventurer sent a chest he
found via POOF to a guild shop that had purchased or financed the quest's
resulting goods. The chest was ornately inlaid with mythril and quite
valuable on its own. Instead, the damaged and broken weapons and armour it
contained came out **alloyed with mythril**. **[C]** Discovered by accident,
in the field, not in a workshop — which is why the practice spreads as a
story guilds tell each other.

**[P]** Mythril is rare — a law of the genre; a full mythril set is a
late-story rarity. **Mythril alloy gear is worth more than standard and less
than full mythril.** So the shop bought a mythril chest and scrap, and
received a scrap chest and mythril-alloy arms. **[P]** Whether that trade
came out ahead is a math problem — at what quantity does the gear outweigh
what was paid for an ornate mythril chest, which as a collectible may be
quite valuable. **[C]** Neither figure is solid, so both sides can do the
arithmetic honestly and disagree.

**[P] TLC cares when value changes in its custody.** *"Sir, why does the
manifest say scrap weapons?"* The rate and the liability rest on the
manifest, and POOF has made it untrue in transit. Whether the chest was
**cargo** or a **custom container** decides what was charged and what the
co-op is answerable for.

**[C·ok] Who worked it out: the call center.** Two calls — the shop asking
what it has been sent, the adventurer wanting someone to answer for the
chest — neither holding the answer. An agent with both accounts open sees
that the missing mythril and the new alloy are the same mythril. R&D learned
it from the floor, not the other way round. The exploitation starts from the
story spreading, not from any notice.

**[P·C] How the player receives it.** Not as a document: a coworker shares it
in an encounter, after the player's first POOF-related call — *POOF is great,
but there's this weird quirk; sometimes the enhancement or repair goes
sideways, curves, or confuses nearly everyone.* **[C·ok]** The coworker's
version is **already drifting** — they weren't on that call either. The gist
is true, a detail or two aren't. That is why agents' basics differ from one
another, and why an agent can be certain of something that isn't so. The
player lives under the same memory rules as the customers.

**[C·ok] Existing upgrades.** They **are** the item's nature, so they are
always taken into account — that is what decides whether an item leads and
how far it can carry a partner's nature. They are **never erased, but they
can be altered**: a matching nature steps the upgrade up a tier; a differing
nature changes its character rather than replacing it. A flaming sword sent
with something cold comes back still enchanted, with a flame that behaves
differently — not always the way its owner wanted. **[C]** If POOF could
strip an enchantment nobody would send an enchanted item; if it only ever
improved them there'd be no gamble. Altering keeps both, and gives the
customer a real complaint that isn't a loss: *it's still a flaming sword,
and it isn't the one I had.*

**[C·ok] The maker — covered by existing rules, not a new one.** A
craftsperson's skill is part of what makes an item's quality, and quality
sets the strength of its nature: whether it leads, how far a partner's
nature can reach, how strongly it takes the enhancement. **[P]** Not every
piece a maker produces is a masterpiece, and masterwork can't be replicated
on demand. **[C]** §9's rule again — improvement grants reliability, never
permission. Skill sets the range, the piece lands where it lands; an
apprentice can occasionally exceed their usual and not repeat it. So a
masterpiece is recognised afterwards, never commissioned, and *"a master's
sword"* isn't a single value. **[C]** Economically, better craftsmanship
gives better POOF results, so POOF sends work to the crafting guilds rather
than undercutting them — fitting for a co-op that serves every guild.

**[P] "Custom Container" is a line** on the invoice, the order, and any other
relevant record. It is the player's indication to possibly dig further.

**[P] Why the line exists: a fee.** TLC uses mass-produced, generic, boring,
uniform containers — the weight of one is the weight of another, so any
additional weight is the contents. A custom container weighs more, the
overall weight increases, and a Custom Container fee is necessitated.
**[C]** So the line is billing doing its job, not a warning marker the
co-op added to catch anyone.

**[P] It is innocuous.** It could mean something; it doesn't have to lead the
agent to continue progressing the customer's request. Every shipment could
carry the line; every shipment carrying it could mean something to the
request; and every request it could mean something for doesn't mean it
applies to **this** request. **[C]** So it can't become a checklist item —
checking every time wastes the customer's time, never checking misses the one
that mattered. Knowing when to look is judgement, like knowing when POOF
isn't the answer at all.

## 2. Cards

**[P] The opening is free.** The greeting and the universal question are
forced, so they cannot cost one of the nine.

**[P] Identification is two plays** — ask her for something searchable, then
search on it. The record deals nothing until she has given something.

**[P] Order matters inside a turn.** The first card searches the record on the
primary identifier; a second handle played after it is the agent checking the
record against details she has already given. Both are searches; what differs
is who retrieves.

**[P] A statement is an inquiry.** A confirmation that invites a No: yes
confirms in one beat, no corrects you and hands you the right thing anyway. A
run of them reads as guessing at everything.

**[P] A find is a card in hand, not a turn.** The hand stays at five, so every
candidate carried is one fewer fresh card dealt, and a fix can be paired with a
question. **[P]** A find is also playable in the moment it arrives — the chain
carrying on is not a separate play — at the cost of acting on the first
candidate, unverified.

**[P·C] The aside** — asking over your shoulder while they are still on the
line. Costs a play, returns less than a brief, cannot be refused, and costs
the caller a little because she hears you not knowing. It is what a call has
instead of a hold when the caller cannot be put on one.

**[P] A record is a hierarchy, and a list shows its rows.** Opening a category
puts its rows on screen; reading a row adds what is not on the face of the
list. A list that returns a count is a blank screen with a number on it.

**[P] An answer puts its own content in play.** What follows necessarily from
it is never gated; what she volunteers on top is gated on how the call is
going. **[P] Her side of the deck is single words**; only the record's own
field labels may be two.

**[P] Nine plays.** The game inherits the training game's budget (Appendix):
the agent has **nine**, and asking the customer something, asking a coworker
for help, or looking something up each uses one.

**[C·ok]** It is what makes *offers are paths with edges* (§6) load-bearing.
With nine, an inquiry has to do work: set the boundaries, keep the customer's
answer inside them, and drive toward progress — **transfer, hold, or resolve**.
A question whose answer was already on the agent's own screen costs a ninth of
the call.

**[C] Consequence for the written calls:** they were written as dialogue and
justified afterwards, never played against the budget. Sella's ORGANIZED path
spends nine by *"From?"* — the moment she realises — and the drafted ending
sits entirely outside it. Each of the hand-written calls needs replaying to
nine before it can be a standard for anything.


**[P]** Playing cards should be simple, without too much thinking.

**[P]** The model is Marvel Snap's Draft mode: you have cards, and as you
work through an interaction you can draft a new card or upgrade an existing
one, and sometimes draft an already-upgraded card.

**[P]** The challenge is keeping cards simple enough to work without them
seeming oversimplified for the sake of upgrades.

**[P]** Runs nest. A match is a run inside a Draft run; a call is a run
inside the run of a day.

**[P]** Combos don't have to be played at the same time. They can be played
in sequence, with a better cumulative effect.

**[C]** Validate or Expand plus a target is what the agent does; the card is
the move as it's actually delivered.

### The six suits and the four angles

**[P]** The base cards are six suits: **Who · What · Why · When · Where ·
How**.

**[P]** **Verify · Validate · Expand · Contract** are the four angles to an
inquiry. **Random** is the player not making a distinct choice and letting
the RNG gods run wild.

**[C]** So Random is abdication rather than a mechanic — the apprentice who
doesn't know which angle and says something anyway. It has to be genuinely
available, genuinely sometimes fine, and worse in expectation. It also gives
the card work a baseline it lacked: if choosing deliberately doesn't beat the
dice over many calls, the angles are decoration, which is the **Flat**
pathology in `docs/trust-and-verify-cards.md`.

**[C]** Verify and Validate differ in what they check against: Verify against
a source, Validate against the person. Validate is where the
statement-as-inquiry lives.

**[P]** Reasons are the other side of the coin from facts.

**[C]** So a *Why* is verifiable through its consequences rather than
directly — the record cannot confirm a reason, but it holds the facts that
must be there if the reason is real. Verify returns **corroborated,
contradicted, or silent**, and contradiction is the CRM's distinctive power:
it kills a line of inquiry in one play, where the customer might agree with a
wrong reason just to move things along. Silence is the expensive one.

**[C] "Door" was an analogy, not a term.** Where Validate and Expand are
called doors (§6, §7), the illustration is that verification and validation
of provided information can lead to progress. **[C] Withdrawn, mine:**
reading it as a mechanic sitting alongside the suits.

### Targets

**[P]** The targets are the customer's **minions** — the enemies the player's
character fights. Key words and CRM categories are both targets; a CRM
category triggers it to populate the results.

**[P]** The real decider of the results is the target:

- Inquire with the customer, **the customer decides**.
- Inquire with the CRM, **the CRM responds**.
- Inquire with a coworker, **the coworker decides**.

**[P] The player is the catalyst** for the way it is presented, and the
perception of the player's choice impacts the recipient's response.

**[C]** So skill splits in two: whom you aim at, and how you deliver it. A
mis-aim is a miss however well it was said — asking her what the record holds
reads as not having read it, asking a coworker what the CRM holds reads as
laziness. Delivery is live for the two targets with a mind and inert for the
CRM, whose defence is structural rather than attitudinal: know the category,
pay a play to read the row.

**[P]** The number of targets is irrelevant. The player is attempting to
attack the right targets from within the noise — almost a three-dimensional
version of Battleship, except there are targets and there are Targets and
there are TARGETS, and probably some in between.

**[P]** A CRM is a hierarchical structure. Hitting one target might link back
directly, indirectly, tangentially, or to red herrings. Working in three
dimensions means there are multiple paths in any direction you can imagine.

**[C]** So the signal is a **kind of link**, not warm or cold, and a
tangential link and a red herring look identical at the moment they are
found. They separate only by whether anything else reaches the same place —
one path to a fact is a lead, two independent paths are a confirmation, and
that is a judgement the player makes rather than a readout the game gives.
Proximity in the hierarchy stops meaning proximity to the cause.

**[C]** Nothing on the board is fake. The noise is genuine records: true,
related, irrelevant.

**[C] Superseded, mine:** that a hit yields a countable number of targets,
that one target per hit and two on a critical was the number to tune, and
that the board needs a slot cap. Yield is structural — what the target
contains — so hitting an Account gives the Account with its children, and a
slot count is meaningless against a subtree.

### One deck, with the board dealt

**[P]** The deck the player owns and the hand dealt from what has been said
are a **single deck**, with the targets being the catalyst making the change
necessary.

**[C]** So what you own is the move — suit, angle, upgrades — and what the
call supplies is the board. The hand mechanics move onto the targets rather
than being lost: two a turn, discard and draw, wear, a find occupying a slot.
Writing a fact down is how it stops taking space on this call's board and
becomes something you own. And the same *Why* is a different move against
her, against the record, and against a coworker, which is how the cards stay
simple without being oversimplified.

### Capability, not reach

**[P]** How do you justify an apprentice not having the six most basic
questions at the start? Having them doesn't mean you have the capability to
use them properly.

**[C]** So reach never changes across a run: all six suits and all four
angles from the start, and the progression is how well what you already hold
actually works — which is what drafting an upgrade is. An apprentice holds
*Why* on day one and plays it badly; it misfires more, lands flat, and she
hears someone fishing.

**[C·note, required by §17a]** This leans on the Held item **awareness,
knowledge, capability**. What the work needed was a distinction between
holding a card and being able to play it, and nothing else in the record
provides it: without it there is no account of why an apprentice who owns
every question is still bad at the job.

**[C] The counts.** Six in the deck to begin, and six in hand at all times,
since there is no draw. Nothing is dealt from the deck — dealing happens on
the board. Nine plays a call, the opening free, one move per play: one suit,
one angle, one target.

**[C] Superseded 8 October, mine:** *one move per play*. Phil's is one or two
inquiries a turn — see *The turn, and her side of it* below. The card build
in `src/cards/` still plays one, and `docs/plan-cards.md` tags that claim
**[P]** when it was mine.

**[C] Superseded, mine:** that an apprentice starts with a partial set of
suits and angles and unlocks the rest.

### The turn, and her side of it

**[P] A word appears to the player only after the customer or the CRM has
introduced it.** A word nobody has introduced cannot be played upon.
*(8 October, restating what 28 September settled: what can be played is
based entirely on what has happened.)*

**[P]** The player offers **one or two inquiries per turn**. *(28 September:
"play 2, or discard and play 1"; restated 8 October.)*

**[P]** The choices available to the customer are **0, 1 or 2**: she
responds to **neither, the first, the second, or both**. **[P]** Neither is
also what Phil called *the random option*. **[C]** Corrected 10 October: Claude
had been writing this as "1, 2 or both" and treating her answering neither as
a fault to be reduced, when it is one of the choices.

**[P]** The customer chooses whether she responds to **the first, the second,
both, or the random option**. Her choice is based entirely on her character
sheet — roughly like an AI or an actor given a role.

**[P]** The random option is where a bad-experience template, a frustration
template, and any number of non-sequiturs can be generated, which creates a
sense of variety and the unexpected. **[C]** Those three are examples of a
wider set, not the list.

**[P] Why it is needed.** As built on 8 October her reply did not depend on
what was asked: *"So no matter what I ask I get the same response? That's
boring."* and *"There's one right answer and six wrong ones. That will become
repetitive and memorized quickly."*

**[C]** A turn is one of the nine whether it carries one inquiry or two.
Mine, from Phil's "nine turns" on 28 September.

**[C]** Which inquiry she takes up is itself information, so steering (§8)
happens on every turn and not only on a miss: taking the second and leaving
the first is her pointing.

**[C]** Pairing is then the player's skill, and it is *offers are paths with
edges* (§12): two inquiries are A and B, and when she goes to C the player
judges whether C sits inside them.

### Her side, mirrored — *tentative*, 10–12 October

Phil's, folded in 12 October as tentative additions. Sources, with the

**[P]** Asking the customer to confirm something she herself introduced
confirms nothing — *"The customer introduced something, so without confirming

opinion asking it back is idiotic. *(He was using* verify *as the plain word,

**[P]** *"The inquiries to an available 'target' word have to make some sort

**[P]** Perhaps the inquiries trigger a handful of remembrance words, in

**[P]** The hand of five is the **player's**. The customer's side has not

**[P]** Her side should be a similar experience to the player's cards —

customer's perspective, not a second machine beside the first.

**[P]** She has the same six cards and chooses which aspect she answers from,

**[P]** All six work for any target: *"Who for a payment: I paid it. He paid

*"I can think of several ways it works."*

**[P]** Her checking her own memory may give confident wrong memories,

**[P]** A working rule rather than a rule of the game — what Trust and Verify

### Critical

**[P]** Leaning toward: a critical is a target that is an improved option.
Instead of just a PO, it displays some options that PO could align with —
Repair, Order, and so on.

**[C]** So a critical removes noise rather than adding material, which is
where the difficulty actually is. It generalises across the three classes

CRM a handle arrives with some of the request types it touches, from her a
word arrives with what she connects it to, from a coworker a lead arrives
with the cause it points at.

**[C]** Some of the options, never all, and never which one is right. Show
them all and one lucky roll ends the hunt, which is the **Solved** pathology.

**[C] Superseded, mine:** that a critical means depth, the exact words
instead of the gist, or something the target would not normally give up.

## 3. Upgrades

**[P]** Pick one effect per upgrade. A card can be upgraded up to three
times with the same upgrade, or take two upgrades with one of them upgraded
a second time.

### The three categories

**[P] Armour**, **Strength** (critical) and **Finesse** — Finesse a working
name, replacing "Disguise."

**[C·ok] Armour** — protects how a *mistake* is taken. A misfire genuinely
lands less badly; a real reduction, never the same damage hidden.

**[P]** Armour has three jobs: soften a miss, help recover from a debuff
already in effect, or provide a path back from a future one.

**[P] Armour by level:** level one does **one** of the three; level two does
**up to two**; level three does **up to all three**. Which ones apply depends
on the current interaction — with no debuff in play, recovery has nothing to
do.

**[P] Strength** — a normal hit, with a chance of a critical.

**[P] Finesse** — rephrasing, alternative positioning and redirection, to
better position the action. Like a status effect or aura; an alternative to
the defensive and offensive upgrades.

**[C·ok] Finesse** shapes how a *choice* is taken, and that carries into
memory. Armour is for mistakes, Finesse for choices; only Finesse reaches
back.

**[P]** Finesse sits on the card it's attached to. Finesse on a No card
unlocks the pivot from a standard No to the better No.

**[P]** An aura can linger into the next card and reverberate against what
came before it. Finesse adds a curveball to the fastball-and-changeup of
Validate and Expand.

**[P]** Reverberating backward eases an earlier hit; it never erases it.

**[C·ok]** The upgrade level decides how big Finesse's boost is. The
customer's sheet decides how far it carries.

**[P]** Reassurance is an aura — a type of Finesse. It softens the past and
sets you up for success in the future.

### Individual upgrade effects

| Effect | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Reliability** — lands as meant more often | **[P]** Yes |
| **Visibility** | **[C·ok]** Yes — see below |
| **Durability** | **[C·ok]** Yes — see below |
| **Speed** | **[P]** No |
| **Yield** — draws out more | **[P]** Yes, dialed down: it increases the odds in the player's favor and must not produce a consistently effective card |
| **Reach** — works on more kinds of customer | **[P]** Yes, and rarer |
| **Softer misses** | Became Armour |

**[C·ok] Visibility.** Hesitation is written into the words, never annotated.
Without the upgrade the player gets the gist; with it, the exact words. The
line stays up until the player's next move — never a timer — then collapses
to gist. Scroll-back is gist only, unless the player noted it.

**[C·ok] Durability.** The card gains another way of saying it, so reuse
sounds less like a script. It still wears down. It hides repeated words, not
a repeated move.

**[P] Empathy and Durability interact.** Higher empathy may produce higher
durability — though empathy can be viewed in a negative light by some
customers, in which case a warm agent repeating themselves wears out faster.

### Debuffs

**[P]** A misfire debuffs that card and adds an effect for the rest of the
call.

**[P·from DSC]** It also makes the customer a **worse source**: they stop
volunteering things, answers get shorter, and small details drop out.
**[P]** Recoverable within the call — steadying them or repairing it brings
the information back.

### Repetition

**[P]** Repetition is generally viewed negatively. Minority edge cases —
hand-holding, gold-glove, do-it-for-me — appreciate it, look for it, or want
it.

**[P]** The last-three-turns check applies to both the door and the subject.

**[P]** A third check of the same thing is dangerous for anyone. For a
hand-holding customer it's *sometimes* acceptable — never guaranteed.
It depends on that customer in that moment.

**[P]** A new apprentice is **softly** limited, not hard-limited: they can
repeat a door, it simply works noticeably better to alternate. Improving
narrows the gap.

## 4. The customer remembering the player

**[P]** Not an upgrade, and not under Strength. It's an effect triggered by
the player's performance, **according to the customer**.

**[P]** What that memory is depends on that customer's memory. A repeat call
can grow the relationship as customer and player become familiar.

**[P]** Effects can reach a single round, the next call, or **further into
the future** — no horizon.

**[P]** The rate is small. Earning space in someone's memory as a call center
agent is harder than it seems: you might speak once and never again, and the
player never knows what impact they had.

**[C·ok]** So the game never confirms it. No tally, no notification. The only
evidence is a caller who rings back and opens differently, and most of the
time that never comes. The player's reason for working well can't be the
payoff, because the payoff is invisible and usually absent.

**[P]** Recognition can come through manner or through the record, and
depends on the player, the customer, or both. The customer can be wrong about
the player; the player can be wrong about the customer, and reviewing history
helps confirm it.

**[C]** Correct a mistaken memory when being wrong changes what happens next.
Let it go when it's only about the past.

**[P]** *"So let's make sure we've got the right date/order/case/request."*
There's a mismatch, someone is wrong, and we're in this together either way.
No blame, focus on progress.

## 6. How calls are built

### The experienced agent

**[P]** Recognizes the request and identifies an immediate resolution; asks
for context to establish the timeframe for a solution; recognizes that
because it worked in the past, a subscription issue is unlikely.

### Fixating on a detail

**[P]** A new agent can fixate. Examples: the hostile (are you safe, can you
retreat, hide, attack); the enchantment (what is it, is it effective
against…, do you need it immediately, payment, subscription); the item
(purchase location, status).

**[P]** The caller may hear someone who isn't listening, is listening to the
wrong aspect, or is focusing on the wrong thing. Which read they make is
theirs, from their sheet.

**[C·ok]** Mechanically, fixation is a move that leaves nothing for the next
move to build on.

### Request and Need

**[P·from DSC PERSON v1.0]** The Request is what they're calling about. The
**Need** is what would actually make them whole. **[P]** Every call has one,
and whether it matches the Request depends on the scenario.

| | Request | Need |
|---|---|---|
| Sella | Why didn't it refill? | A healing draught tonight |
| Ivo | Is it meant to do that, can it go back? | A cloak he can wear |

**[C·ok]** The Request card is handed over in the first ten seconds. The Need
has to be **earned**, using the doors we already have — Expand into what
they're trying to do, or Validate a guess at it.

**[C·ok]** It changes three things: what counts as a resolution (this is the
mechanism behind a correct, fast, complete call that still leaves someone
unsatisfied); what a coworker can help with, since a Need card in the
description changes the advice; and what the better No has to pivot to —
Ivo's two options land because they answer his Need, not his Request.

**[C·ok]** The agent can never tell from outside whether the two match, so
surfacing it is always worth doing, and often just confirms what was said.

### Reading drift **[C·ok]**

There is no catalogue of tells, because the same behaviour means opposite
things in different people — going quiet is one person thinking, another
being polite, and a third deciding they are done.

What a player reads is **a change from how this caller was five turns ago**.
The signal is the deviation, and it exists only relative to that person. It
shows as behaviour, never as a gauge, and how much of it an agent catches
depends on their visibility and what they have grown into. It is also why it
cannot be taught as a checklist — you get good at the shape of a thing, not
the thing.

### Two channels

**[P]** Agent-to-customer and COOP-to-agent are separate conversations.

**[P]** Search results give the agent context and options: **Billing,
Account History, Order History, Subscriptions, Party Members** (authorized
users). **[P]** A party has an **Admin or Leader** with greater privileges
than a standard party member. **[P]** *Minions* is one customer's own word for
the same thing — a villain prefers it and will not be swayed — which costs the
accuracy of the record rather than the call.

**[P]** Registration is an irrelevant detail — required before an account,
let alone a subscription, could exist.

### Continuation, progress, automatic responses

**[P]** One card flows into another: search, results found, results shared as
the reason for what happens next. **Telling** fits inside continuation rather
than being its own aspect.

**[P] Progress is the chain continuing** — Action A drives Reaction B drives
C — Improv's *"Yes, and…"*. It is **not** a separate scored step.

**[P]** Lines like *"There you go"* and *"Glad to hear it"* are automatic
responses.

**[P]** *"Turn it off, turn it on"* — sheathing and redrawing — is a general
card. There's a version of it for anything, from the right perspective.

### Writing

**[P]** Paraphrase or summarize; never parrot verbatim. Customers describe
problems — the request card is the agent's understanding of it. A coworker's
best line points the agent at the right next step. Don't import concepts from
the previous iteration.

## 7. The Hold

### When a hold is right

**[P]** The sword call had no need for a hold.

**[C·ok]** A hold is right when the apprentice truly doesn't know, someone
else on the floor does, and nobody is in danger.

**[P]** Whether a caller accepts a hold depends on their part in the moment.
Someone fighting refuses; a party member who can't help against these enemies,
or someone calling before or after the battle, may accept.

**[P]** When nothing has been shared that allows a search, and the apprentice
couldn't interpret what a search found anyway, guessing is not the path.

**[P]** The same moment can trigger other plays: Expand (switch the line of
inquiry), Validate (actively seek knowledge), and others.

**[P] Transfer to a different department.** **[C·ok]** The better No is the
Finesse on the Transfer card.

**[C]** The departments a call can be transferred to are listed in §1.

**[P]** *"Our expert"* is a safe, customer-friendly catch-all for checking
with a human resource rather than a documented one.

### What a Hold card needs — **[P] all six stay**

Which of them matter depends on how that customer responds to each.

1. **A reason.** It's always *for* something.
2. **The caller can say no.**
3. **A cost that depends on the moment** — not time, but how they take being
   held.
4. **A way of asking** — where Finesse goes.
5. **Coming back** — the end of the chain, where telling happens.
6. **Wear** when repeated.

### Coworker conversations

**[P]** It's Mad Libs. The apprentice describes the case using **fact cards**
earned during the call:
- **Name** (Customer Card)
- **Confirmed Details** — with the customer
- **Verified Details** — from a system search. RNG for first-time callers;
  the sheet records an encoded reference for later searches.
- **Stated** — said, never read back or recorded. That it wasn't indicates
  something, and it may be left out of weaker descriptions. *(Label mine.)*
- **Paraphrased Customer Request**, improving over time:
  **Oversimplified, Simplified, Generalized, Organized**

**[P]** Template: `[Customer] [InteractionType] with a request for support
with: [ContextDependentScenarioDescriptionTemplate]`

**[P]** Coworkers redirect or add context. They aren't omniscient, and their
help depends on the description.

**[P]** Explaining doesn't cost hold time — it's cozy. A speed-run version
with a real clock would.

**[P]** Verified cards are one fact each; a call can carry more than one
Customer Request card.

**[P]** Causes are rolled each time. A coworker only knows the causes they're
familiar with, or have vague knowledge of.

**[P·C] Availability is a variable.** Bram is not a constant. He may be busy,
gone home sick, or nobody may be free at all. Whoever takes the question knows
the causes *they* know, so the same call resolves differently depending on who
was standing there. **[C]** The agent is the carrier between them: a second
conversation is with someone who didn't hear the first, so whatever the first
coworker said exists only in the agent's brief — §18's knowledge flow arriving
inside a single call.

**[C·ok] When nobody can help:** the agent says so and **it becomes a case**.
Not a guess, which is already ruled out, and not an escalation, since nothing
has gone wrong. The customer is told plainly that nobody who knows is free,
that it's going on record, and that someone will come back to them — the async
queue doing its job. It buys Certainty and Time-and-effort at the cost of an
explanation today.

**[P] The path always exists.** Every brief leaves a route to the cause. What
varies is how hard it is to find: which coworker answered, what their brief
pointed at, what the customer volunteers, and whether the agent connects what
they hear. A weaker brief makes the path longer, never absent.

### Coworkers have sheets too

**[P]** The coworker is similar to a customer: they have a character sheet,
they have a **preferred method of communication**, and they have **baggage**
too.

**[C]** So the brief is not the whole input. An Organized brief delivered the
wrong way still bounces — someone who wants it in three words resents the
full account, someone who wants the story treats a summary as being fobbed
off. The Paraphrase ladder gets the material; their preferred method decides
whether it arrives.

**[C]** And baggage means a coworker has a trigger as well, which is what
stops a coworker being a lookup table. The apprentice is a coworker to them,
with a sheet of her own.

## 8. The customer sheet

### It grows

**[P]** They're RPG characters with stat sheets. They grow.

**[C·ok]** So it isn't DSC's frozen static layer plus a changing one. It's
**one sheet where different things change at different speeds**:

| | Rate |
|---|---|
| Mood in this call | turn to turn |
| What they know | when someone teaches them, and it stays |
| How they feel about the co-op | over several calls |
| What they make of you | with repeat contact |
| Personality | barely, and only after something big |

**[C·ok]** The seed stops being a guarantee and becomes what it was useful
for: a cheap way to make a first-time caller without writing anything down.
Once they've called, the sheet is their record.

**[C·ok]** The rate matters more than the ability. *"What worked last time
doesn't work now"* only lands when most of the person is still the person.

### It misremembers

**[P·C]** Option **C**: the register stores **what they took away** — feeling
and gist get written, exact words and details mostly never make it in — and
time-sensitive things keep slipping afterwards (*"three weeks ago… I
think"*). Loss happens at the moment, plus a little drift on dates and names.

**[C·ok]** The player's side needs nothing new: their memory is the case note
they wrote, and a history that reads as gist unless noted.

### What gets written back

**[C·ok]** A moment is written **if it was felt**, with how much feeling it
takes decided by that customer in that moment. An ordinary competent call
writes nothing.

**[P]** One moment **can** write to more than one register at once — better
about you, worse about the co-op.

**[C·ok]** Only the **residue** carries between calls, never the stack. A
caller who nearly walked last time arrives sourer, and the lining-up starts
fresh — carrying the stack itself would mean remembering the specifics
perfectly, which they do not.

**[P]** A later call **can** remove an earlier impression, and doesn't have
to. New experience can also be layered on top, so they carry both — *"they
got it wrong, then they put it right."* Repair is worth doing and is never a
reset button.

### Emphasis, exceptions and records **[P]**

Found while working Sella's call. Applies to customers generally; how it
lands is decided by the specific customer.

1. **Placement shapes what can become the gist.** The same offer, worded
   with the favour first or the outcome first, gives the customer a
   different thing to hold onto. Spoken and written placement pull
   differently. Which part a given customer actually keeps, and how they
   feel about it, depends on their sheet: history, baggage, memory, what
   they're braced for, and what they came in needing.
2. **A poor choice of emphasis often shows nothing at the time.** If the
   customer reacts, the agent can recover on the call. If they don't, their
   version is written and surfaces on a later call, set against the case
   note. Whether it was felt at all, and how strongly, is that customer's to
   decide.
3. **Records are a second imperfect memory.** Exceptions should be
   documented, but in practice some go unlogged, logged thinly, logged in
   the wrong place, or logged differently from what was said.
4. **Cozy rule for exceptions: nobody is punished for kindness.** The cost
   of an exception is the expectation it can leave behind, and that comes
   back through people, not penalties. Whether a given customer carries
   that expectation forward depends on them.
5. **The budget is conceptual.** It explains why exceptions are limited. It
   isn't a visible meter, which retires the earlier *discretionary allowance
   draining across a shift* raise.
6. **Honest error, both levels at once.** Even with proper instructions,
   people and a person make mistakes — the prisoner's dilemma from a cozy
   perspective: both sides cooperate, and it can still go wrong without
   anyone defecting. At the **PEOPLE** level, forgiveness is given, which
   keeps it cozy. At the **PERSON** level, the mistake can still add to that
   customer's baggage. What it costs is what it costs that player and that
   customer in that moment — never a penalty the game applies.

### The win categories on the sheet **[C·ok]**

The fifteen categories themselves are design, not data — the same for
everybody, so PEOPLE. What the **sheet** holds is the **weight** of each one
for this person: Sella came in braced for someone to guess at her, so
Certainty and Treatment sat high before she said a word; another caller with
the same empty flask wants Access and nothing else, because they need a
draught by noon and don't care why.

**Not one ordering** — fifteen weights with fifteen rates, the way §8 already
works. Safety is set by the situation and can be high today and absent next
month. Treatment is close to permanent. Opinion shifts over several calls.

**Several appear twice.** Goodwill and History are account facts in the CRM —
what was given, what was recorded — *and* weights on the sheet: whether she
feels owed, and what she remembers being told. The two are not guaranteed to
match. The courtesy granted and never logged is exactly that gap: she arrives
expecting it and the record says it never happened.

**[C·ok] Where the weights come from — settled 26 September.** The categories
are not a block on the sheet; each weight is read from what the sheet already
holds, and changes at the rate of whatever it comes from (the table above).
**[P]** Baggage carries much of it: everyone arrives with history and a sense
of goodwill about a company, whether they've called before or only heard.
**[C·ok]** Baggage also sets Treatment and Certainty — Sella, braced to be
guessed at — and the situation sets Safety and Access. **[C]** Personality
sets the slowest, such as preferring a rule to a favour.

### Fields

**[P]** **Assignment is luck of the draw.** The player has no selection
over who they get.

**[P]** **End customers are individuals.** An end customer may also be an
employee of an organizational customer. The relationship between the two is
employment; the classes do not merge.

**[P]** All rolled per customer, and all able to change at their own rates —
tolerance for repetition in particular can wear down inside a single bad
call.

**[P]** Each trait gets a **written meaning at low, middle and high**, with a
continuous value underneath: the number decides the band and the strength
within it, the band decides what it means. Three is the default, not a rule.

**Tolerance for repetition**
- *Low* — notices a repeat at once and reads it as not listening.
- *Middle* — wears normally: fine once, thinner twice, plain by the third.
- *High* — wants it. Varying things leaves them unsure anything was settled.

**Empathy** *(the customer's own)*
- *Low* — the agent isn't a person to them. Warmth doesn't land.
- *Middle* — extends goodwill on an obviously busy day, but isn't looking.
- *High* — reads the agent closely, may let a floundering apprentice off the
  hook — and notices a technique delivered without meaning it.

**Pattern sensitivity**
- *Low* — hears none of it.
- *Middle* — catches a straight run of the same thing.
- *High* — hears shape: alternation, two-then-one, three-then-one.

**Tolerance for non-positive outcomes**
- *Low* — a couple of misses and they want someone else.
- *Middle* — absorbs a fair bit, and shows it.
- *High* — absorbs a great deal and stops signaling. You lose your read, and
  they can ring off unhappy without saying so.

**Depth or breadth — baseline [P]**
- *Prefers digging* — switching the subject feels like being brushed off.
- *No strong lean* — either works, as long as it's going somewhere.
- *Prefers casting wide* — dwelling on one point feels stuck.

A starting position only. A failed attempt, or the agent's last move, pushes
it from there.

### The engine

**[P]** DSC's personality mix is **dropped** — our fields do the job.

**[C·ok]** The chain from a card played to the sheet changing:

1. **The move** — door, target, card, upgrades.
2. **The customer's filters** — what they want now, whether the thing checked
   was said with certainty, the repetition and pattern check over the last
   three turns, whether they resist this kind of card, their empathy.
3. **The landing** — critical, lands, flat, or misfire. Reliability widens the
   good outcomes; Armour softens a miss; Finesse carries in.
4. **What they show** — visibility decides how much the player perceives.
5. **What it leaves** — the reaction, a debuff if it missed, an aura carrying
   forward, and whatever the next move can build on.
6. **What gets written** — if felt, to whichever registers it was about.

### Wards, and the trigger for a Need

## 9. The player

**[P]** The player is an **apprentice**, working through the call center to
become a journey-person able to work for a Partner on their master projects.

**[P]** Which Partner they are aiming for gives us **classes** to choose
from. **[C]** A class is a destination rather than a stat block — it says
where you are heading, not what you are capable of.

**[P]** Traits — empathy, charisma, authority and others — **grow through
gameplay**, not from the class.

**[P]** An apprentice who works beyond their expertise can fail. Suggesting a
transfer to the proper specialists earns some experience; getting it right
beyond your expertise is lucrative in experience terms. **[C]** The sting is
losing the call, not a penalty applied to a number.

**[P]** Improvement grants **reliability, never permission** — a new
apprentice may attempt anything; it simply works less predictably.

### How traits grow — and slip **[C·ok]**

The same rules as everything else, pointed at the player. Generally, traits
grow by doing the work, by things landing or not landing, and by what a
coworker or supervisor gives you — all available to anyone. Which of those
actually moves *this* apprentice is specific to them; the same call teaches
one and not another.

The mechanism is the customer's, reversed: what persists is what was
**felt**, so an ordinary competent call leaves nothing behind. It takes
**several things lining up**, not one event. The **rate is small**, and the
game **never confirms it** — you find out you have grown when something
lands now that didn't before.

Traits slip by the same rule. The manager who blames you twice is several
things lining up against escalating, and what moves is not a score, it is
you. Which things line up for a given apprentice is theirs; the same shift
wears one player down and leaves another untouched.

## 10. The CRM

**[P]** A hierarchy of hierarchies:

- **The Company** that has the CRM
- **The Accounts** the Company has authorized
- **The Contacts/Users** under each Account, at two levels of access —
  **Admin** or **User**

**[P]** The Account has visibility to all sub-records, which may or may not
be visible to a User. **Admins see all. Users see only what they created or
were assigned by an Admin.**

### Admin, Members, and the resolved conflict

**[P]** An Account must have a Contact designated as the **Admin (Party
Leader)**, for the other Contacts who are Party Members.

- An end-user customer could have an Account and a single Contact (Party
  Leader).
- An end-user customer could have an Account and Contacts, but only **one**
  Party Leader.
- A Guild customer would have an Account and Contacts, only one of them
  **Guild Leader** — the guild-specific Party Leader designation.
- An organizational customer would have an Account and Contacts, only one
  Party Leader.

**[P]** Organizations can be Kingdoms, Castles, Strongholds, Baronies,
Fiefdoms, Stockades, Militias, Mercenaries and so on.

**[C] This resolves the conflict with §17a** rather than choosing a side.
Contacts sit under an Account, exactly one of them is Admin, and *the Account
has visibility to all sub-records* is **what the Admin sees** — not a second
parent. Visibility is by access level, as above, and the two routes to a
sub-record are two **scopes**: the Admin's account-wide view and the Member's
ownership-scoped view. The multiple paths that search depends on survive as
scopes plus cross-category keys, rather than as two parentages.

**[P]** A **PO number** is a multi-category customer-provided variable. It
may appear across Accounts, Contacts, and whichever child they could be
related to.

**[C]** So one probe on a customer-provided identifier returns several
*paths*, not several rows: the same PO under two Contacts is two routes to
different children.

**[C]** One Admin per Account also makes the honest, unsatisfying No below
structural rather than incidental — a Member who cannot see their own party's
order has exactly one person who can, and may not know their own party's
rules about it.

### The Bag, and the party's privacy

**[P]** An Admin can assign items to a User, moving them from the **Party's
Bag of Holding** to the **Member's Inventory**.

**[P]** The Party Leader / Member designation is TLC's own invention. Each
Party can determine their own rules for it, and TLC is blissfully unaware of
what those rules are.

**[P]** TLC considers a Party's Bag to belong to the **Party**. A customer may
feel differently — the party's own concept of privacy about what is in the Bag
of Holding. **[P]** Which has a chance to bite TLC in the butt, triggering an
**Angry** caller. Cozy game: they may know, or they may not.

**[C]** So privacy here is not a permission setting. It is a mismatch between
TLC's model and the party's, and the agent is reading from a record that is
correct while saying the wrong thing out loud. Nothing in the CRM can warn
them, because the party's rules were never told to TLC.

**[C]** That gives an Angry opening with a source we did not have: not the
agent's mistake and not the record's error, and not repairable by better
searching. It can only be handled.

**[C]** It also reaches the make-up options. Options 4 and 5 are made from
what is in the shared inventory, so a custom order can tell one member what
another member put in the Bag.

**[P]** Sub-records include **Orders** (order numbers, purchase order
numbers, confirmation numbers, order date, shipment date, invoices, packing
slip numbers, statuses) · **Repairs** (repair numbers, confirmation numbers,
purchase order numbers, submission date, shipment date, statuses) ·
**Tracking** (tracking numbers, statuses, delivery date, shipment date,
expected arrival, return reason) · **Returns** (return number, return date,
refund status, refund amount, refund date, exchange order number, exchange
status) · **Case** (case number, status, open date, close date, notes,
subject, descriptions, history).

**[P]** Below that is the level of detail that makes **one** record specific
from others in the same category. The generic similarities feed the naming,
which leads to misinterpretation through oversimplification or
misunderstanding when seen from inside a single support case.

**[P]** The agent can see anything they can find **with the right piece of
information.** The wall is not permission, it is having a key.

**[C]** This is where *searchable versus usable* comes from. Confirmation
Numbers sit under Orders and Repairs; Purchase Order Numbers under both;
Shipment Date under Orders, Repairs and Tracking; statuses under everything.
A Return Number points at one record. The ambiguity is real rather than
authored.

**[C]** It also produces a cause we did not have: a **User who genuinely
cannot see** their own organization's order. Not misremembering — not
permissioned. And with it a No that is honest, blameless and completely
unsatisfying: *"I have it here, I can't go through it with you, but your
Admin can."*

## 11. The case — the second half of play

**[P]** The player's work is in two halves. Card play is the first. The
second is a **Mad Libs case template.**

**[P]** Filling a slot defaults to the **generic descriptor**, which is
always available and always the fallback.

**[P]** Slot options come from a pool of valid searchable or usable adjacent
terms. **Searchable and usable are not the same property** — a term may be
one, the other, or both. An Order Number slot offers options such as Order ·
Order Number · Number · Purchase Order · PO Number · Confirmation ·
confirmation number, and other valid order-adjacent terms exist beyond those.

**[P]** A slot can render twice. Repeating or varying is the player's choice.

### The memory layer

**[P]** Which options the player sees comes from card play, rendered through
a **memory layer that distorts which options appear** — not their
presentation, order, or apparent certainty.

**[P]** This is distinct from the customer-side memory unreliability. That
governs what the caller retains; this governs what the player is offered
when writing it up.

**[P]** It **can** surface options that were never actually heard. Memory is
flawed — people misremember others' names because the brain feeds them
flawed information.

**[P] "Noting" has two senses, and they are different acts.** During the
interaction it means *noticing* and investigating — attention, in the moment.
Afterwards it means *creating a note* as the record of what happened. The
memory layer sits between the two: what you noticed feeds what you are
offered when you build the record, and it distorts on the way across.

**[P]** Trust and verify still applies — a wrong note is catchable. And
having made a note says nothing about whether you also remember it perfectly;
you can verify a number and still write it down wrong. **That is why multiple
options matter when completing notes or communication templates:** a mismatch
between what you noted and what you recall is visible in the slot, and that is
the prompt to check before it goes out.

**[C]** So phase two is the last place verification can happen, rather than
transcription.

### The async case and the team queue

**[P]** A case is **a word problem** in the asynchronous interaction. The
second half of that gameplay is assembling the appropriate communication back
to the customer, much as the case note is assembled for a phone interaction.

**[P]** What the player is reading may be the customer's written thread, or
another employee's, or **multiple perspectives at once.**

**[P]** Asking a question **holds the case** while waiting for a response. It
climbs the queue — FIFO — whether a response arrives or not. The player has
to decide at what point no answer is coming and resolve it, or what happens
next when one does.

**[P]** A supervisor can run reports and start asking questions about cases
they consider too old. A good median point is **a week — five business
days.**

**[P]** Cases are worked **when the line is quiet**, in FIFO order, with live
interactions weighted more heavily — roughly 60/40.

**[P] One task at a time.** Assigned work takes priority, period. You cannot
and should not have two interactions at once.

**[P]** A live interaction concerns either a **New Issue or an Existing
one**, and the player has to determine whether a case already exists and act
accordingly. An asynchronous interaction may be a **duplicate**, or a request
for an **update** on an existing case.

**[C]** Which closes a loop: the case notes the player writes become the word
problem another agent has to read. The four description levels stop being a
grading scale and become what lands on a colleague's desk.

## 12. Inquiry shape

**[P]** Forming an inquiry involves having an idea of what the player wants
to accomplish — pointed, generic, broad, detailed, and so on. It is the
difference between open and closed questions, **or something in between: a
confirmation that invites a No, with an expansion on it for when the Yes
doesn't work for the customer.**

**[P]** What happens next when the reaction isn't what you aimed for is its
own question.

**[C]** The choice is a guess about two things at once — what you want back,
and what this person can give you. Asking in the CRM's own vocabulary is
precise for us and opaque to them.

**[C]** The in-between form makes being wrong cheap for both sides: yes
confirms in one beat, no corrects and hands you the right thing anyway. It is
the mirror of the better No — the agent handing the customer an easy No to
*give*. Overused, a run of *"it's this, isn't it?"* reads as guessing at
everything.

**[P] Offers are paths with edges.** Structure a response in anticipation of
questions or pushback: questions choose between paths A and B; pushback lands
against targets A and B. The goal is to guide the conversation through
boundaries. If the customer answers with C, check whether C sits within A and
B — if it does, keep making progress; if not, reframe A and B.

**[C·ok]** A reaction that misses is itself information. Confusion tells you
what they know; defensiveness tells you what they think you meant.

## 13. Escalation

**[P]** One of the two doors out of the loop. Either side can reach for it.
**Manager escalations are the consequence** of a failed attempt beyond the
apprentice's expertise.

**[P]** The apprentice **does not know who is taking it until someone picks
up.** It is a gamble every time.

**[P]** What the escalation *is* depends on the kind of manager who answers.
**[C, except where noted]** The kinds: the one who takes it and the
apprentice is off · the one who coaches, keeping them on the line and often
handing it back · the one who repeats what the apprentice already said, with
authority · the one who gives in to end the call, reversing the apprentice's
No in front of the customer · the one who asks what you've done first · the
one who sends it back with instructions · the one who shields and never
mentions it after · the one who isn't there · **[P]** and the one who takes
it and blames the apprentice afterwards.

**[C·ok]** That last one changes the player rather than the call. An
apprentice who meets it twice stops escalating, including when they should.
It is the clearest case of something tipping the *agent*.

**[P]** **If no one is available, it becomes a callback**, logged in the case.
It's the same interaction moving from live to asynchronous, with a request to
go live again with a manager, supervisor, lead or senior staff. The apprentice
has to deliver it, and it's another No to find the better version of.

**[P]** Whether an escalation leaves a mark depends on that specific
customer.

### What a customer's feelings can attach to

**[P]** The Agent · the Supervisor · the Product · the Service · the Company.

**[C]** Also worth carrying: themselves, when they felt foolish · whoever
they bought from · their own organization, when their own Admin is the
obstacle · a previous agent specifically · support in general, before we say
a word · what it has cost them to get this far · and whoever else is
affected, when the thing was for someone.

## 14. Influences and framings

**[P]** Four influences: the prisoner's dilemma · the trolley problem ·
finding a better No for this customer when No occurs · the Mad Libs case
template, built on using the best option available, which is viewer
dependent, as perspectives vary the way people vary when they are viewed as a
person.

**[P]** The better No sits in **card play**, not the template.

**[P]** The trolley problem parallels the better No: sometimes No is the
answer, and finding the better version is easier said than done. **[C]** It
is why the better No is hard — there is no version where the customer gets
what they wanted, so the choice is which harm.

**[P]** Everything in moderation. **[C·ok]** But moderate is not a fixed
middle — the dose belongs to the person, which is PEOPLE and PERSON wearing a
proverb. **[C]** The exceptions are the things that aren't technique: never
landing blame on the customer has no moderate amount.

**[P]** Like rock, paper, scissors — each has a weakness, and until both
players choose you don't know what happens next. **[C]** Two differences: the
customer isn't choosing a counter, they simply are what they are; and unlike
a throw, the match-up can be changed mid-call, which is what Finesse does.
The analogy carries repetition well — throw the same thing three times and
you get read.

### Win-win-win, by whose view **[P]**

**[P]** "Best" is not a property of a scene; it depends on the perspective.
The audience viewing determines the Win-Win-Win — the customer's, the
co-op's, the agent's, the supervisor's, the team's, the team's organization
within the co-op, the next point up the hierarchy, the Council, the head of
the co-op. In real life a true Win-Win-Win often devolves to a specific
Win-Win, or a single specific Win, classified as good or good enough — which
is where grey areas and knowledge gaps form.

**[P] The Council** — the co-op's duty to its members is visibly met, the
tiers keep their value, and it is **profitable**. Everything shipping equally
all the time would be boring. **[C]** Unequal treatment between tiers and
accounts is a feature: the same problem can be two different calls.

**[C·ok] The team's organization** — the first version (*fixed at source, on
schedule*) was rejected as forced and imaginary. Replaced by Coverage and
Apprenticeship in its list below: the work gets covered without any one team
drowning, and apprentices come out able to do the job.

### Sella's possible wins **[P·C]**

At the moment she decides to contact support — flask empty, nothing said yet,
before any conversation. **[C·ok]** Her win-win-win holds on only one path;
elsewhere it devolves to a win-win, which is her own principle above. A win
she never learns about is not a win: *not feeling foolish about drinking it*
only exists if she finds out she did.

**[P·C] Fifteen categories of win**, from her side, at that moment:

1. **Product/Service** — the thing itself works: a draught today, refills
   tonight, repaired or replaced if broken.
2. **Certainty** — knowing where she stands now, so she can plan her day.
3. **Money** — no extra cost, and something back for the night she paid for.
4. **Time and effort** — quick, one person, no repeating, no chasing.
5. **Knowledge** — why it happened, how to prevent it, something new about
   the co-op.
6. **History** — the call is on record; anything wrong on her account gets
   put right while someone is looking.
7. **Treatment** — listened to, taken seriously, believed, helped, **not made
   to feel foolish for calling**.
8. **Opinion** (of the co-op and/or its employees) — she thinks better of us
   than she did, and the subscription feels worth it.
9. **Safety** — the risk of being without a healing draught is closed, or at
   least named.
10. **Access** — a solution is reachable from where she is.
11. **Control** — she can do something herself rather than wait on us.
12. **Privacy** — helped without having to explain why she needs it.
13. **Fairness** — a rule applied rather than a favour granted.
14. **Goodwill** — credit in hand for next time.
15. **Reliability** (of product/service) — she trusts the flask again, going

the rule exactly as it's applied to everyone (Fairness without Treatment).

once, and separate as soon as a call goes sideways — which is where the game
lives.

### Account and Contact as co-parents **[HELD]**

**[P]** Every account should have at least one individual associated with it.
Account and Contact are **co-parent variables**. Accounts can see all cases,
orders, shipments and so on associated with them. A Contact can see everything

account-wide view is the Admin's view rather than a second parent, and the

of those two scopes; it is no longer a competing structure.

**[C]** *What it would change if used:* the same search returns different sets
depending on which was opened, so an agent can be on the right account in the
wrong scope — looking at a customer's problem and not seeing the order that
explains it, because another party member created it. §10 already sets
visibility by access level; this would set it by ownership instead, which is
the conflict noted above.

### The Portal, and Admin or User **[HELD]**

**[P]** People use the **Wizard Fidelitas** network — WiFi — to reach

### Awareness, knowledge, capability **[HELD]**

**[C]** Read as three separate facts about a person: aware of it, able to do

## 18. The shape of play **[P]**

Given 23 September as an analogy for the shipping container example and for
every customer service request at once.

**Which buckets hold the answer.** Which support "buckets" contain the
valuable information needed to progress toward a solution, **for this
specific customer and their specific request**? Everything is available;
only some of it is relevant to this one.

**A request can change type.** A customer presenting one type of request may
turn out to have meant a second, third or fourth type by the time a
resolution is found.

**Perception of the request depends on the viewer,** whoever that is.

**Knowledge creep and knowledge gaps.** Businesses track a wide variety of
information and it lands in the CRM. Because of departmental overlap, fields
available to one viewer mean other things to another viewer — which leads
directly to knowledge creep and knowledge gaps.

**The player doesn't know what they know, nor what they don't know.**

**What the loop is.** Exploring to figure out the customer's request, and the
need to guide, support and provide "good" customer service — *good* being
subjective and dependent on the receiver's feedback, in the moment verbally
or non-verbally, or after the fact — **is the player battling with their
cards.** Completing the case is the **after-action report**, capturing what
you learned.

**What happens next is random:**
- another call interaction is assigned
- another case interaction is assigned
- a supervisor interaction begins
- a teammate interaction begins
- a co-worker interaction begins (R&D or other divisions' employees)
- a break or meal begins (which could lead to a co-worker interaction)

**Interactions with fellow TLC employees can share:**
- TLC lore
- POOF updates from R&D
- card enhancements
- card upgrades
- customer lore — "legendary" or "boss-level" characters?

**Identifiers are repurposed across categories.** Purchase Order is the clear
example: Orders, Shipments, Invoices, Returns, Repairs and others all use
Purchase Order numbers in some way as a "valid" identifier. Order Number is
often reused, or reformatted with an added word to make it "unique" from
other order numbers, and then colloquially shorthanded back to Order Number
or an acronym.

**"Customer" is another.** A customer contacts the call center. Is the
customer a Customer · a Partner · an Employee · a Potential Customer ·
a Potential Partner · a Potential Employee · a Former Customer · a Former
Partner · a Former Employee · a Pending Customer · a Pending Partner · a
Pending Employee · on the correct support path · even **our** customer?

**If you can't identify the customer, what leads you to believe you can find
the appropriate support path for them?** It is a lousy assumption to classify
one type of customer as equal to all of them. The same logic holds as you
dive into the categories a request may fall into.

**Five categories of identifier [P]:** Account · Contact (User) · Case ·
Related Numbers · Related Details. Details is more for the **case** than the
investigation — important nevertheless.

**[C]** Account and Contact answer *who*; Related Numbers answer *which
thing* (order, shipment, invoice, purchase order, tracking, repair); Case
answers *which conversation*, and is the only one the co-op creates during
the call rather than finds. Related Details describe rather than locate: a
number narrows to one record, a detail narrows to a pile, and details confirm
a record something else found. The customer almost always arrives holding
details and almost never a number, so the agent's work is turning what the
customer has into something the system accepts — directly, or by stacking
details until they narrow to one.

**[P·C] When the customer hands over "the number."** It doesn't ruin the
game; identification is the door, not the room. Three versions, used
deliberately — a **change-up after a few fastballs**:
- **The gift** — right number, short call, genuine relief in the rhythm. The
  co-op wins on time and effort; the agent learns nothing, which is fine.
- **The number that doesn't help** — correct, and it identifies a record
  where the problem doesn't live. A match plus false confidence is worse than
  nothing.
- **The wrong-category number** — read out with total certainty, resolving to
  a record that isn't theirs or isn't this one.

**[C]** Who has numbers to hand is characterisation: business accounts,
partners, admins, frequent shippers, and people who have been let down before
and now keep records. Sella had a name and a description.

**Baggage shapes the telling.** Customer baggage is a factor in the
explanation of any given request. One of many examples: being burned before
by a wrong department on a previous interaction can influence the
explanation, the emotions, the tone and the language on the next one.

## 19. The ordering call **[P]**

**[P]** An end user rings to order a POOF Instant. Net new shipment. Nothing
has gone wrong, so there is nothing hidden to find: what there is instead is
what has to be settled before it ships.

**[P] Two stages, one budget.**
- **Discovery** — identify what is actually wanted.
- **Fulfilment** — entered once the request is identified as a POOF Instant.

**[P] Arranging is free; questions still cost.** Laying out the makings while
she is on the line is doing the work, not an inquiry. Any question asked
during fulfilment spends one of the nine — like ordering at a counter, where
walking the line is free and *black or pinto?* is a question.

**[C·ok]** So banking plays is the skill, and a question asked during
fulfilment is better than the same question asked at the start, because the
choice is in front of her and she can answer it properly.

**[P] The player is dealt the ingredients** and arranges them to ensure a
proper delivery. **[P·C]** Dealt, not chosen from a full list: stock varies,
and a substitution can be **offered at a stated price** — she agrees to it or
accepts the thinner result, so nobody pays more than they expected.

**[C·ok]** What it costs instead is exposure: she hears every choice. A player
who never established her urgency or her budget is improvising audibly.

**[P] Quantity** — a second of something is a second set of ingredients.
**[C]** One container shares a roll between them; two containers cost more
carriage and keep them independent.

**[P] Switching what is being sent is free** — it is the customer's choice.
**It does not reset the nine**, so plays spent on the order she has abandoned
are gone.

## Appendix — Support Call Practice *(training game, approved)*

**What it is.** Three trainees work one customer's problem together, then
each writes up the case. Trainees take turns being the agent, so everyone
runs a call.

**Roles.** One trainee is the **agent** and runs the call. The other two are
**teammates**; they hear the whole call and can help when asked. Someone
plays the **customer**.

**The call.** The agent has **nine questions**. Each of these uses one:
asking the customer something, asking a teammate for help, looking something
up in a resource. Once all nine are used, the three trainees work the call
out together.

**After the call.** Everyone writes their own case — the agent and both
teammates. You all heard the same call, so compare how each of you opened the
case and wrote the notes.
