# Lore — the Translocation & Logistics Cooperative ("TLC")

Everything below was established through conversation, not invented
unilaterally — where something is a proposal rather than a locked
decision, it's marked as such. See "Open Questions" at the end for
what's genuinely still undecided.

---

## The Company Name — Locked

**The Translocation & Logistics Cooperative ("TLC")**. Every word is
literally true of the company — it moves things magically
(translocation) and physically (logistics), structured as a co-op —
and TLC falls out as a bonus, the same shape as "COOP for the Future"
and "COG": the real name comes first, the acronym is earned, not
forced. Replaces the placeholder "Wyrdline Support Co-op," which never
survived its own naming critique earlier in this document.

The company's public identity leans on its flashiest line of
business — magical shipping — the way FedEx's name says nothing about
customer service either. Support is the unglamorous backstage the
player actually lives in; the company's brand is about the glamorous
part. That's an honest detail, not a mismatch.

**POOF** is the branded name of the premium direct-ley-hop shipping
tier (see Logistics & Shipping Tiers) — not the company itself.
Tagline, locked: *"When your package needs more than TLC, pick POOF.
It's there before you blink."*

## The Merger & Founding

Two guilds shared one **town** for centuries — not a headquarters, a
town, each running their own separate operation the whole time.

**The town's name, locked: the Village of Import.** Double meaning,
both halves earned: literally a trade/logistics hub across two very
different eras — ships arriving pre-merger, ley-line goods arriving
post-merger, "import" true the whole time — and "of import" in the
sense of *significant*, despite "Village" staying modest in scale.
"Port" sits quietly inside "import" too, the same kind of echo as
Newport or Bridgeport. Fits the running theme of understated
importance already established elsewhere (COG, Leadership's
officially-neutral-actually-just-nice building) — a Village, not a
City, quietly the birthplace of an accidental monopoly.

- **The Mage Guild** — the old proprietary magic model: individually
  licensed spellcasters, billed per-incantation. That model didn't
  collapse overnight, but subscription-based ley-line infrastructure
  made it unsustainable — nobody pays a certified enchanter when the
  ward just *works*, as a utility. What it meant in practice was a
  managed reshuffling of guild staff, driven as much by the guild's
  own duty to its members (and its own interest in members staying
  able to pay dues) as by any external pressure.
- **The Maritime Guild** — owned the realm's shipping lanes, ports,
  and physical cargo routes. Reach was limited to wherever ships and
  rivers could physically go — but they extended that reach inland,
  slowly, through **authorized Partner Couriers**: independent local
  businesses, not Maritime Guild members, licensed to display the
  Guild's logo on their premises and clothing in exchange for
  handling local delivery from a port or river terminus. The logo
  carried real prestige for the courier's own business, not just the
  delivery income. **This means the Co-Op's "Partner" tier isn't a
  new invention at the merger — it's this existing Maritime practice,
  extended to cover ley-line-enabled delivery too.**

  **Culture, locked**: salty sea dogs, blue-collar, work hard and play
  hard. Leadership saw the change coming — "it was on the wind" — and
  knew merging would bring in the coin the guild needed to keep
  functioning at all. That was a hard practical call from the top, not
  a shared conviction: rank-and-file members went along with it, but
  the Co-Op still feels unnatural to a lot of them. Different shape
  from the Mage Guild's transition, which leadership actively managed
  *for* its members' sake — this one is leadership doing what had to
  be done, and the culture underneath never fully came around to it.
  Fits, and reinforces, the already-established "Arcane side / Maritime
  side never fully integrated" detail.

**Why they merged, in order:**
1. Subscription magic makes the Mage Guild's old per-incantation
   model unsustainable → a staffing question the guild has to solve
   for its own members, not a sudden crisis.
2. To give the surplus staff somewhere to go, two new things get
   created: the **call center**, and a **research division**.
3. The research division discovers that since ley lines now connect
   settlements, outposts, kingdoms, cities, forts, and barracks, the
   network can carry *physical goods*, not just magic/service.
4. That discovery is what drives the deal with Maritime: existing
   Partner Couriers (and new ones) take on ley-line-enabled last-mile
   delivery alongside their existing local work. These Partners
   generate their own support tickets too — running a delivery node
   is its own operational headache. **"Partner" is a role, not a
   business type**: it's the same Tavern/Inn/Blacksmith/Potion Shop/
   Wandering Trader businesses already established as Business-tier-
   eligible commercial entities, but classified as Partner specifically
   when the interaction is about their courier work — inventory moving
   through them, commission payouts, their listing in the network —
   not generic shop-keeping.

   **Two sub-roles within Partner, locked: Node and Courier.** A
   **Node** is a place — it receives and holds shipments, a waypoint
   in the network. A **Courier** is an actor — it physically moves
   shipments between places. Both are formally "Partner," but in
   common parlance, internally and externally, nobody says the full
   term: everyone just says "the Node" or "our Courier." A single
   business could hold either role, or in principle both, depending on
   its specific arrangement with TLC — a Kingdom garrison serving as a
   pickup point is a Node without necessarily doing any of the
   courier's legwork itself.

   The existing three ticket categories already split cleanly along
   this line, without having been designed with the split in mind:
   `Storefront Listing Error` and `Inventory Sync Issue` are Node
   problems (something about the waypoint itself); `Commission Payout
   Dispute` is a Courier problem (payment for legwork done). Which
   *future* ticket categories belong to which role isn't fully spec'd
   — see Open Questions; this pass locks the naming and the
   distinction, not a new ticket-category system on top of it.

   (This is *why* "Partner Vendor" is a
   customer tier in the existing game design, distinct from Business.)
5. A new customer type falls out of this naturally: businesses who
   *aren't* Authorized Couriers yet, contacting support to ask how
   to become one. A sales/onboarding conversation, not a support
   one — distinct from an existing Partner's support call. Flagged
   as a future ticket category, not built yet (see Open Questions).

**Maritime's motivation**: vertical integration. Their courier network
already reached inland, but slowly — relayed hand-to-hand from
whichever port was nearest. Ley lines did the same job in one direct
hop. Maritime bought speed, not access. The resulting monopoly is
**accidental** — nobody merged to conquer anything, it was just the
obvious move once the Mages figured out how to reach everywhere more
quickly and safely than the Maritime Guild was capable of doing.

They were also protecting their own guild members and partners.
Agreeing to the merger kept dues flowing, too — the same duty-of-care
motivation that pushed the Mage Guild toward it in the first place.

## Ley Lines

- **Natural state**: wild, random, and organic — like nature itself.
  Patterns *have* been found, but they don't always hold, and
  sometimes contradict each other outright. Understanding them takes
  real, ongoing study — the Research division is actively working on
  it now, not just the one discovery that enabled goods transport.
  (Water, landmarks, and celestial alignment were all tested as
  candidate explanations early on and ruled out — the search moved
  on, it didn't stop.)
- **Bent** by the Mage Guild to align with trade routes — for
  commercial convenience *and* safety. A concrete example: it's hard
  for monsters to attack travelers when a ward is already in place
  preventing it.
- **The toll/ward mechanic**: conceptually a toll. Pay it, and the
  ward applies to you *and your current method of transportation*.
  Change transportation methods mid-journey — swap wagons, switch to
  horseback, whatever — and the ward doesn't automatically follow;
  it has to be re-set on the new method. Tolls are also scoped to the
  **group**, not the individual: if you split off from the party that
  paid, you're not covered anymore. The toll paid for the group as a
  whole, not each person in it — leave, and you need your own.
- This artificial bending makes them **less stable** than they'd be
  naturally — not wildly unstable, just imperfectly settled.
- That instability is the literal in-world source of "glitches" —
  Respawn Delay, XP Miscount Dispute, Border Ward Malfunction, and
  similar ticket categories are the signature of ley lines that were
  forced off their natural path and never fully settled. The
  toll/ward mechanic above is a natural source of these: a ward that
  doesn't cleanly follow a transport swap, or a group that splinters
  without realizing coverage split with them, reads as exactly this
  kind of glitch from the customer's side.
- **Important**: the player does not need to know this connection.
  It's background causality — ours to know, not something the game
  explains. Nothing about the *ground* is unstable; only the lines
  themselves, and only because they were bent.
- **Network growth is ongoing, not a one-time founding event.** As
  new ley lines reach new places, new towns get added to the route
  network, and new outposts get founded that can grow and develop
  over time on the strength of that connection. Each newly bent or
  rerouted line carries its own fresh instability, separate from the
  founding-era lines — meaning a specific, newly-connected location
  can run higher-than-normal ticket volume for a while, until its
  line settles. Unlike the existing `SHIFT_MODIFIERS` (which are
  global, one-shift-at-a-time), this is *location-specific* and
  *lasts across many shifts* — closer in shape to the Figurehead's
  standing policy layer than to a per-shift modifier. Not yet
  implemented; a good candidate for how the game world could expand
  post-launch without it feeling arbitrary.
- **Deep timeline, resolved: this is all still very new.** The
  Research division has only just gotten the ley-line network stable
  enough to call reliable, and the safety wards (see the toll/ward
  mechanic above) are only now coming online — not a settled,
  generations-old institution, an active rollout still in progress.
  This recontextualizes several things already established rather
  than contradicting them: the Research division's inconclusive
  findings, patterns that "don't fully make sense yet," Maritime's
  rank-and-file never fully warming to the merger, COG's fresh envy —
  all of it reads as more coherent once the whole premise is recent,
  not decades-settled. The player is effectively present near the
  ground floor of something historically significant happening in
  real time, not inheriting a mature bureaucracy's long history.

## Logistics & Shipping Tiers

Five tiers now, not three — modeled on real-world logistics economics,
each serving a genuinely different route shape or use case rather than
just being a speed/cost slider on the same trip.

1. **Direct ley-hop (A→B), branded "POOF"** — fastest, priciest,
   straight line, one hop. Tagline: *"When your package needs more
   than TLC, pick POOF. It's there before you blink."*
2. **Hop + local courier** — one ley-hop (node-to-hub, or hub-to-node)
   plus one courier leg (pickup or drop-off) on whichever end isn't
   already sitting on the network. Two steps total. Cheaper than a
   full direct hop.
3. **Priority Relay** — courier, then hop, then courier: three legs,
   for routes where *neither* end is near a major node. Local pickup
   carries it to the nearest hub, an express hub-to-hub ley-hop covers
   the long middle stretch, local courier finishes the last stretch
   at the far end. Distinct from Tier 2, not a rename of it — Tier 2
   only needs courier work on one side of the trip; Priority Relay
   needs it on both, and specifically buys ley-hop speed for the
   long-haul middle rather than defaulting to a slow full relay.
4. **Caravan** — the "Ground/Freight" equivalent: significant cost
   savings for large, regular bulk orders (a Business-tier account's
   standing monthly supply run is the classic case), at the cost of
   speed.
   Route is cost-optimized per shipment — ley-hops, physical wagon
   transit, or some mix, whichever is cheaper for that specific bulk
   load. Physical transit usually wins on raw cost even at scale, but
   not always; it's a real choice made per shipment, not a fixed
   method.
5. **Courier (relay), a.k.a. "the safest physical path"** — no ley
   line at all. Moves town to town, a new courier taking each leg of
   the journey, the same shape as a pre-airline postal service. Slow,
   but cheap, efficient, and the everyday baseline for letters and
   small packages. Genuinely called "safest," not just a slower
   fallback: couriers travel established trade routes that carry
   **ambient ward coverage** from the original bending-for-safety work
   (see Ley Lines above) — coverage tied to the *route*, not to a paid
   toll. Paying the toll layers stronger, personalized protection on
   top; going without still means monster-warded roads, just the base
   level. So "tiered delivery pricing" is functionally tiered *safety*
   too, not only speed and cost — which is why this, the slowest and
   cheapest option, is a reasonable choice, not a reckless one.

*(Wildline routing — riding a wild, un-bent line, fast but genuinely
unpredictable — remains a separate, not-yet-achieved candidate; see
Ley Lines and Open Questions. It needs a Research division breakthrough
that hasn't happened yet, unlike the five above, which all already
work.)*

**Why anyone chooses the slow options** (Tier 5, and often Caravan —
multiple, stacking reasons):
- Cost — they're cheaper, and still reasonably safe (see above).
- Some goods are volatile or magic-sensitive and may not survive ley
  transit — or they will; the Research division's findings are
  inconclusive so far, same as their work on natural ley patterns
  generally. Nobody's actually certain, which is its own reason for
  caution.
- Some goods deliberately avoid ley lines to dodge the registry
  trail (contraband).
- **The Co-Op complies with "the Realm's" restrictions** on
  undesired groups/items moving through ley lines — thieves,
  murderers, henchmen, poisons, drugs, ill-gotten gains. The Co-Op
  didn't choose to be the arbiter of this; it's a condition of
  operating the network.
- Purely personal reasons — including simple distrust of anything
  new or different.

**Villain vs. restricted criminal — locked.** A Villain is the *hero*
of their own story — nobody considers themselves the antagonist; their
nemesis is the antagonist, from their own point of view. That's a
narrative role, not a legal status.
The call center doesn't treat a Villain customer any differently than
anyone else, provided they're respectful. The Realm's restrictions
(above) are about actual conduct and goods — thieves, murderers,
poisons, contraband — not about which archetype someone happens to be.
**Company policy is behavior-based, not alignment-based.**

That's a claim about *treatment*, not about who the character actually
is — a different axis, not a contradiction. The archetype label still
legitimately shapes a character: their emotional stability, what they
know, how they react to an answer they don't want to hear, their
perspective, and how they share information. It's one trait among
several that compose a character, and those traits influence each
other rather than just adding up side by side — see "Archetype rhyme"
under Customer Service Philosophy, which is this same principle
stated from the character side rather than the player-skill side.

**One-sided accounts, as a real design lever.** A Villain customer
tells their side of a conflict — the player may never directly hear
their nemesis's side of the same story. If that Hero later calls in
as their own customer, the player's only prior impression of them
came filtered through an adversary's account. This makes "Trust and
Verify" (below) concrete rather than abstract: trusting what a
customer says happened *to them* is one thing; a customer's account
of *someone else* isn't verified truth just because it's sincerely
believed. Fully developed as "Multi-Angle Recurring Characters," its
own section below.

## The Realm

Deliberately left as just "the Realm" — no invented proper name (see
Open Questions history). The ruler is known as **"The Unseen."**
Nobody knows whether they're even still alive; laws and proclamations
keep issuing regardless, and nothing suggests bad or tyrannical rule
— just total absence from public life. This is the reason the Realm's
restricted-goods policy (below) has always read as impersonal and
administered rather than decreed by anyone visible: there's a real
in-world explanation for that now, not just an unnamed governing body.

## Screening & Classification ("Pigs is Pigs")

Screening is **hybrid**: the ley-line registry does something crude
and automatic — flags anomalies, refuses certain material categories
outright — and then a *person* has to interpret and act on the flag.
The interesting tickets aren't "did the magic work," they're "was the
flag right."

Direct inspiration: the old *Pigs is Pigs* scenario — a rigid, literal
tariff classification (a pig is a pig, livestock rate applies,
regardless of context — including to someone's guinea pig). Applied
here, the ambiguity is often multi-way, not just one wrong answer: a
Fire Drake could get flagged as Livestock, Transportation, *or*
Hazardous Cargo, and a rigid clerk (or system) could defend any of the
three.

A pet mimic can hit the same plain misclassification problem — flagged
as Hazardous Cargo or as Livestock, either defensible on paper — but
it also exposes a sharper flaw underneath: **screening checks the
label, not the truth of the thing wearing it.** The crude,
automatic layer asks "is this a person, and is that person flagged as
undesired?" — it never asks "does this actually verify as what it
presents as?" So a mimic disguised as an ordinary traveler clears the
undesired-groups check easily, not because mimics are some blind spot
screening forgot about, but because that check was never built to
question whether a "person" is a person at all. The same trick works
on cargo: a mimic disguised as a shipping container gets one cheap,
legitimate item declared and priced, while the "crate" itself — a
live creature — rides along unpriced and unclassified. Same underlying
flaw, two different things being smuggled: itself as a passenger, or
something else as cargo. Neither is a *wrong* classification to
dispute — nothing was flagged as anything, which is the whole problem.

The agent decides whether to enforce the letter of the rule or
exercise judgment — real risk either way, since the rigid rule is
sometimes actually protecting against something real.

Maps directly onto the existing resolved/hold/transfer mechanic:
- **Resolved** — agent rules on the classification themselves (right
  or wrong, it sticks, or it gets disputed later).
- **Hold** — kicked up for manual review.
- **Transfer** — punted to whoever actually owns tariff schedules.

*Not yet built*: this hasn't been turned into an actual ticket
category/archetype in `gameLogic.js` — it's design intent, not code.

## The Coalition of Guilds ("COG") — Locked

**Not rivals or competitors** — that framing is wrong and replaced.
Still running the old individually-licensed, per-service billing
model, and still operating under a literal, ancient, unamendable
Charter, in contrast to the Co-Op's living bylaws everyone argues
over — but the relationship isn't opposition. "The chartered guilds"
survives as the informal, lowercase way people actually refer to
them day to day, the way "the Co-Op" is shorthand for whatever the
company's own eventual full name turns out to be.

**They love the concept, hate the practice.** They admire the
underlying idea — subscription-based magic, ley-line logistics,
vertical integration — enough to wish they'd been the ones to think
of it first. What they dislike is how the Co-Op actually operates.

**The money is not adversarial.** The Co-Op makes more than COG
outright — but COG's member guilds *also* make more than they
otherwise would, simply because a realm with better logistics is a
richer realm generally, whether or not you touch the Co-Op directly.
They're not losing to the Co-Op. They're benefiting from something
they didn't build and don't fully approve of, which is a much more
specific resentment than competitive rivalry — it's envy, not
survival.

**Locked**: this shapes how COG-affiliated customers behave when they
contact support — some mix of performative superiority and quiet
dependence on the thing they're sneering at. Not written up as
concrete dialogue yet, but the emotional shape is settled.

**They're also high-volume direct customers**, not just indirect
economic beneficiaries — an organization their size means the Co-Op
handles real, regular business with them. And because of that size,
it's rarely the same person calling twice: whoever's on the line today
may not have the context behind why the account is even reaching out,
let alone what happened last time.

**This generalizes beyond COG** — it's really a trait of any large
organizational account (Kingdom, Military, Town Council, Constabulary
included): the *account* has a persistent history, but the *caller*
is whoever picked up today, and may not know it. That creates a real
asymmetry for the memory system: the agent, via company records, can
know more about an account's past than the person currently on the
line does — a different shape entirely from an individual End User,
where the caller and the history are always the same person. Not
implemented — `recallMemory()` currently treats every customer
identically regardless of whether they're an individual or an
organization. Flagged in Open Questions.

**Naming, locked**: "Coalition of Guilds" is a plain, accurate
description of what they structurally are — a federation of separate
member guilds under one shared Charter — that also happens to spell
COG. The real name came first; the acronym is a bonus, the same shape
as "COOP for the Future." A cog is small, unglamorous, and essential
— which fits a faction that feels overshadowed by something flashier
while still being genuinely load-bearing to how the realm runs.

**Member guilds, locked.** One named guild per trade, with related
specializations folded in as recognized subclasses rather than each
getting a separate peer guild — matches how real trade guilds actually
worked.

- Adventurers
- Taverns & Restaurants
- Blacksmiths, Armorers & Weapon-makers
- Healers
- Shopkeepers & Merchants
- Alchemists
- Cartographers & Scribes (Notaries are a Scribe subclass)
- Beastmasters (Menagerie Keepers are a subclass)
- Scholars (Sages and Loremasters are subclasses)

**AIMS is not a COG member — resolved.** The Association of
Independent Magical Studies is independent of *every* guild, COG
included; it's right there in the name. Individual AIMS members may
personally also hold membership in a trade guild, but the association
itself answers to nobody's coalition. Full description moved to its
own section below.

## AIMS — Association of Independent Magical Studies

Independent of every guild, COG included — the name says so directly.
Individual members may personally hold trade-guild membership
elsewhere, but the association itself answers to no coalition.

Made up of unaffiliated magical practitioners — hedge wizards,
freelance tutors, solo researchers — who never joined the old Mage
Guild and still haven't joined TLC. Smaller and scrappier than TLC's
Research division, plausibly doing comparable work with none of the
funding or institutional reach. A guild-scale echo of COG's own "envy,
not rivalry" relationship to TLC, but for people who chose to stay
independent of *everyone*, not just TLC specifically.

## Leadership & Governance

**Physical structure**: the two founding guild sites remain in the
shared town. Leadership operates out of a **separate third location**
— chosen for **prestige/optics wrapped in an official story of
neutrality**. Publicly: equidistant from both founding sites, so
neither side could claim home-field advantage (technically true).
Really: it also happened to be the nicest available building. Optics
wearing pragmatism as a disguise, not an outright lie.

**The building**: a **repurposed noble estate** — a manor or minor
lordling's seized/sold property, grand but never built to run an
organization. **Renovations are ongoing**, perpetually — a running
background detail (a half-converted ballroom, a permanently-closed
wing, scaffolding nobody notices anymore), and a likely source of
budget/priority disputes between the two founding factions.

This physical and organizational separation — Leadership disconnected
from frontline staff at either original site — is the established
mechanical source of "why isn't this working properly." The specific
incidents that fall out of it aren't written yet.

**The Council**: 7 seats total.
- 2 **permanent** seats — one guildmaster each, from the former Mage
  Guild and former Maritime Guild, unelected, seated by right of
  founding.
- 5 **elected** seats, voted on by Co-Op staff only (not Partners).
- Math matters here: the 2 permanent seats alone aren't a majority of
  7, so every real decision needs coalition-building with at least
  some elected seats. Gridlock and horse-trading are the default
  operating mode, not a bug.

**The Figurehead** — the public face of Leadership — is elected
**from among the 7**, by the other 6, not a separate 8th office.
Direct analogy (the user's own): like the Pope being elected by the
College of Cardinals — a cardinal promoted by fellow cardinals, not
an outside appointment.

**The election ceremony**: ritualistic, sequestered deliberation
until a majority is reached — conclave-style pageantry, kept because
it's tradition, not because it's efficient. Officially named:

> **The Conclave Outlining Operations & Policy for the Future**
> ("COOP for the Future")

"For the Future" is deliberately empty, unfalsifiable, forward-sounding
institutional language — exactly what a real bureaucracy bolts onto a
name to sound purposeful without committing to anything. That it also
spells COOP is presented in-world as a coincidence leadership is
smugly, repeatedly proud of — a joke the staff are tired of hearing.

**Policy consequences**: a newly elected Figurehead can change
day-to-day call-center operations. This should be a **standing policy
layer** — persisting across many shifts until the next Conclave —
distinct from the existing ephemeral `SHIFT_MODIFIERS` (which reset
every shift). *Design intent, not yet implemented.* **Delivery
mechanism now designed**: a guaranteed Coaching meeting on the first
lobby visit after a new Figurehead takes office — see "Meetings with
Supervisors & Coworkers" below. Without that, this layer had no way to
actually reach the player.

Concrete policy levers a Figurehead might pull (as given):
- More coaching meetings between agents and managers.
- Pace pressure — employees expected to work faster.
- Additional mandatory training.
- Policy & Procedure updates (impacting process/steps/actions).
- Specialist vs. generalist structure — agents handle specific ticket
  types only, vs. handling everything, flipping back and forth across
  administrations.

## Running the Desk — Queue, Pace, and Being Measured

**Why this section exists**: everything else in this file describes
*who calls* and *what they want*. This describes *the job* — the shape
of a shift, what's scarce during it, and how the company forms an
opinion of the person working it. Its absence is why the built
prototype reads as a ticket list wearing a call-center hat: there is
no queue, nothing is scarce, and the only feedback is a single
reputation number.

**The governing constraint — cozy, and it's load-bearing.** This is a
cozy game (see the top of `CLAUDE.md`). That is not a softening applied
after the fact; it decides the shape of every mechanic below. The
operational layer exists so the job feels like a job — rhythm,
attention, a sense that time is finite — and *not* so the player feels
hunted. The concrete rule: **operations create texture and
consequence, never failure.** Nothing here should produce a lose
state, a timer the player races, or a number that punishes. A real
contact center's ops layer minus the fear is the target. When in
doubt, the cozy reading wins over the realistic one — and the two
agree more often than expected, because most of what makes real call
center work miserable is the *threat* attached to the metric, not the
metric.

### The blended queue

**Locked**: the player works live calls *and* between-call work,
interleaved — not a pure phone queue, not a pure worklist.

Async work is what fills the space between calls. A call arriving
interrupts it. That interruption is the core rhythm of the shift: the
player is always slightly behind on something, and choosing what to
leave unfinished is the actual moment-to-moment decision. The current
build has no version of this — `generateShift()` produces a flat,
ordered list of N tickets and the player walks it start to finish,
which is why it plays like paperwork.

**Callers waiting is the pressure. Nothing worse than that.** The
player can see that people are holding. That visibility is the entire
mechanism — no anger meter, no abandonment penalty, no failure.

### The candle is the shift, and pace is noticed by people — locked

**The candle has nothing to do with any single call. It indicates the
shift.** Not a budget, not a per-call counter, never spent by the player
on anything. It is the working day going by while work gets done.

**A shift is a fixed amount of day, not a fixed number of calls.** How
many people you get to depends on how you worked — which is arithmetic,
not punishment. A call has a floor (greeting somebody, hearing them out,
closing) and beyond that runs as long as the work took. Nobody is
charged per question; a longer call is simply longer.

*Two earlier versions were wrong in opposite directions.* One priced
individual questions in candle-notches like currency — cruel, and it
billed the player for doing the job. The other burned exactly one notch
per call, which made a careful call and a shallow one cost the same day,
so care was free and there was nothing to weigh at all.

**The queue is not a pressure mechanic.** It communicates how much work
is available, and this is a support centre: **there is always more.** It
is never cleared. The day ends because the day ends.

### Pace is noticed at both ends, and only by people

**Taking too long over your interactions is a reason a manager or a
coworker seeks you out. Blazing through them without asking enough is
also a reason.** Both edges get noticed; the middle does not.

That is the entire mechanism — no meter, no score, no resource. A norm,
enforced socially, arriving as somebody wanting a word. It gives the
Meetings system something real to deliver rather than flavour.

### Measured: defection is genuinely tempting

Run across 60 shifts × 8 seeds, three strategies:

| | closes fast | works properly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interactions handled | **1200** | 931 |
| Rework rate | 38.9% | **5.3%** |
| **Real customers served** | 734 | **882** |
| Feeling toward the agent | 9.3 | **24.7** |

**Working fast handles 29% more interactions and serves 17% fewer
people.** Four in ten of those calls are cleaning up earlier ones.

That is the dilemma, and it is the right shape: **the thing that looks
like productivity isn't.** Somebody closing shallow is visibly busier on
the metric a contact centre actually tracks, and the cost is buried in
rework and in how people come to feel about them. The individually
rational move on the visible number is the collectively worse one.

*Caveat on the pace bands*: they read average call **duration**, not
inquiry count — counting inquiries measures the content rather than the
agent, since a category with nothing to investigate makes anybody look
fast. With nine of ten categories currently offering no inquiries at
all, every average is dragged toward the floor and the bands are
provisional.

**A caller who gives up doesn't disappear**A caller who gives up doesn't disappear — they call back.** This
falls out of machinery that already exists: an unreached caller
becomes a pending follow-up, scheduled the same way holds and disputes
already are (`pendingFollowUps`, `endShift()`). Cozy and mechanically
free — the consequence of a long hold is that the work returns later,
carrying the memory of having waited, not that something was lost.

*Resolved*: hold time feeds the **Experience** axis of the disposition
vector — see "Character Disposition Model," *Disposition is a vector*.
Not Baggage, and not a separate wrinkle. Everything the operational
layer does to a caller (holding, transferring, making them repeat
themselves, making them sit through questions) lands on that one axis.

### Between-call work, and what the record actually is

**Superseded — see "The Five Fenceposts — What Every Call Is Made
Of."** An earlier draft of this section treated wrap-up as a separate
cost paid after each call: rush the note, get a thinner file, pay for
it later. That was wrong, and wrong in a specific way worth recording
so it doesn't get reinvented — it imported after-call work as a
*chore*, which is what it degenerates into in a badly-run center,
rather than what it is when the job is done properly.

The correct version: **the note is the artifact of the call, not a
task appended to it.** Once the five posts are set, writing it up is
showing your work. There is no wrap-up minigame and no time budget
for note-taking.

What remains true and belongs here, on the operations side:

- **Async work is still the other half of the blended queue** — it
  just isn't note-taking-as-chore. What actually fills the space
  between calls is open (callbacks owed, submissions to POST on a
  caller's behalf, follow-ups coming due). Not guessed at here.
- **The record is a claim, not a readout.** Notes are written by
  people and people lie — so quality review means reviewing the
  *call*, not reading the writeup and ticking boxes. This is the
  operational consequence of the fencepost model, and it's what stops
  the QA axis below from being a box-checking exercise.
- **The player reads their own file back**, three shifts later,
  knowing more than they did when they wrote it. That's the returning
  customer's real texture, and it's why this connects to
  `recallMemory()` rather than sitting beside it.

### What the player is measured on

**Locked**: all four of the following, and **they are allowed to
disagree with each other.** That disagreement is the point — a call
can score well on quality review and still leave the customer unhappy,
and both readings are honest.

- **Pace** — how long things take. Partly present already:
  `RESPONSE_QUALITY` carries a `timeCost`, `generateShift()` carries a
  `timeCostMultiplier`, `investigationTimeCost()` prices investigation,
  and "pace pressure" is already a named Figurehead lever (see
  "Leadership & Governance"). Nothing surfaces any of it to the player.
- **Quality review** — someone other than the customer assessing the
  work against the company's idea of correct. This is the metric that
  gives the Supervisor **Feedback** register (see "Meetings with
  Supervisors & Coworkers") something specific to cite instead of a
  vague tone.
- **Resolution that holds** — whether it stayed fixed. Effectively
  free: the reopen-risk follow-up machinery in `resolveTicket()` and
  `endShift()` already produces exactly this signal and currently
  throws it away after displaying a count.
- **Customer satisfaction** — the caller's own verdict, which may
  contradict both the quality review and the disposition score the sim
  tracks internally. The customer's opinion of the call is not the same
  object as their disposition toward the player, and shouldn't be
  wired to it directly.

***The shift summary reports actions taken, never accomplishment —
locked.** Even setting feelings aside, a day-end screen cannot say what
was *achieved*. A transfer is somebody else's work now. A hold is
unfinished by definition. And a resolved ticket is provisional — the
reopen machinery already means it can come back disputed. Nothing is
settled at end of shift, because the work is adjudicated later and by
other people (see "Ticket Resolution," *the game does not grade the
diagnosis*). So the summary counts **calls taken and what the player
did with each**, in language that claims nothing about outcome. A
headline total of "calls handled" is exactly the overclaim to avoid: it
reads as accomplishment and silently folds in every ticket that was
punted or left open.

**The shift summary is about work, not feelings — locked.** A shift
summary reports **calls handled and case dispositions**. It does *not*
report how customers felt, because at the end of a shift nobody knows
that yet: customer feeling is learned later, through the survey chain
below, or not at all, or only when a customer said something out loud
on the call. `endShift()` currently tallies `dispositionTag` counts and
the prototype prints "N customers left feeling X" — **both are wrong and
should be replaced** with handled/resolved/held/transferred counts. The
design handoff's End of Day screen has the same problem in its
`WHAT YOU FILED` panel and should be corrected the same way.

*Cozy framing, non-negotiable**: these are how the company *notices*
the player, not how it threatens them. They feed the Feedback and
Coaching registers and the Figurehead's policy levers — both already
designed — and they surface as observations from named people, not as
a dashboard the player is expected to optimize. A bad week produces a
supervisor who mentions it, not a red number.

### How the player learns what a customer thought — the survey chain

**Locked, and it answers what used to be an open question here.** The
player is not shown a customer's feelings. There is no gauge, no score,
no post-call verdict. What exists is a **survey**, and the survey is
unreliable in four separate ways that all matter:

1. **It may never be completed.** Most aren't. Silence is the common
   case and means nothing in particular — a delighted customer and a
   furious one both mostly just hang up.
2. **It may not be honest.** People soften, exaggerate, punish the agent
   for the company's failure, or praise an agent who did nothing because
   the outcome happened to go their way.
3. **It arrives late.** Not at the end of the call. Not necessarily at
   the end of the shift.
4. **It is filtered before it reaches the player.** Surveys route
   through the Co-Op, and reach the agent through **their manager or
   other leadership** — who decide what to pass on, when, and in what
   framing.

That last hop is the important one, because it plugs straight into
Phase 7's **Feedback** register, where a supervisor already "cites
shift-summary specifics or a vague tone depending on the supervisor's
own disposition toward the player." A survey delivered by a supervisor
who likes the player is a different object from the same survey
delivered by one who doesn't.

**This gives the game three layers of imperfect information about one
event**, which is the core loop's own theme extended to the feedback
channel: what actually happened (the sim knows), what the customer
reports (partial, optional, possibly dishonest), and what the player is
eventually told (mediated by corporate and a manager). The player never
gets the first layer.

**The immediate channel is behaviour, not numbers.** During the call the
customer's state shows in how they speak — see "Character Disposition
Model," where each axis has its own behavioural tell. The Stardew
gift-giving comparison holds: the model runs underneath, the reaction is
what you see, and you learn to read people by watching rather than by
reading a stat. Hiding the model is what preserves cozy; showing it is
what would break it.

### Escalation — sideways and upward, both real

**Locked**: both directions exist, and both cost something.

- **Sideways**, to another team that actually exists in the org, each
  with its own turnaround and its own reputation for competence.
  Transferring to the wrong one bounces back to the player later —
  again reusing the follow-up machinery. **POST is the established
  model for the asymmetry** (see "Partnership & Onboarding
  Inquiries"): a real destination with no phone line, where "transfer"
  can only mean submitting on the caller's behalf.
- **Upward**, to a tier above the player. Leaning on it isn't
  penalized — it becomes something a supervisor brings up, which is
  precisely the trigger the **Coaching** register already needs.

The current build's version — a button reading "Off your plate for
good, however it was handled" — is the single most unrealistic thing
in the prototype, and it exists because nothing was on the other end
of it.

*Unnamed on purpose*: the specific sibling teams and the upward tier
don't have names yet. Per "Naming Methodology," these should be
*derived* — from what the team actually does, or where it sits, or who
founded it — not assembled from a corporate word and a fantasy word.
They haven't come up in play yet, so they aren't named yet.

### There is no knowledge base — and that's a fact about TLC

**Locked**: the player has nothing to look things up in. No procedure
manual, no searchable record, no reference. It is judgment and memory,
built up across shifts.

**This is derived, not a limitation.** Leadership operates out of a
separate building, physically disconnected from both frontline sites,
in a repurposed noble estate under permanent renovation, run by a
7-seat council whose default operating mode is gridlock and
horse-trading (see "Leadership & Governance"). **An organization like
that never finished writing anything down.** The documentation gap is
the same disconnection that's already established as the mechanical
source of "why isn't this working properly."

It also explains something previously left as framing: the "Customer
Service Philosophy" principles are passed frontline-to-frontline,
unofficially, surviving despite Leadership rather than because of it.
**They're oral because there is nothing written.** The folk wisdom
*is* the knowledge base. That section and this one are the same fact
seen from two directions.

Consequences worth stating plainly:
- The fix for "the player can't get anything wrong" cannot be handing
  them a reference to look the answer up in. It has to come from
  investigation (already proven for one ticket), from callers who
  differ from one another (Character Disposition Model, not built),
  and from the player's own accumulated pattern recognition — the
  "player learning across tickets" dimension already described under
  "History doesn't repeat itself, it can rhyme though."
- **Policy & Procedure updates**, already a named Figurehead lever,
  land differently in a company with no written procedures: they
  arrive through people — a coaching meeting, a coworker mentioning
  it — and can therefore arrive garbled, late, or contradicting what
  the last administration said. That's a feature.

Not built — design only.

## The Five Fenceposts — What Every Call Is Made Of

*(Adapted from Phil's own agent-training model — eleven years working
and training call center agents. Like the "Customer Service
Philosophy" principles below, this is lived material, not invented for
the setting. Where the two overlap, this section is the structure and
that one is the folk wisdom that grew on top of it.)*

**The claim, and it's a strong one**: after a while all the calls are
the same. Not the content — the content varies endlessly — but the
anatomy. Every call has the same five components:

| Component | Covers |
|---|---|
| **Request / Requestor** | Who & What |
| **Investigation** | Where, Why, What, & How |
| **Support** | Scientific method to solutions |
| **Education** | Teach and share knowledge |
| **Sympathy / Empathy** | Be kind, supportive, understanding, and respectful |

### They are fenceposts, not steps

**This is the part that's easy to get wrong, so it's stated flatly**:
these are **fenceposts the agent builds around the request.** The
request sits in the middle. The five are what the agent sets around it
so it holds a shape.

They are therefore **not** a sequence, **not** a checklist the agent
is marched through, and **not** five parallel meters ticking along
underneath the call. They're a **layout**. All five posts are placed
by the time the call is done; the order they go in is read off the
ground.

- An upset caller: **Empathy first**, because nothing else will stand
  until it's set. Solutioning at someone who needs to be heard is
  driving a post into mud.
- A calm caller who already knows what they want: **Investigation
  almost immediately**, Empathy a light touch, placed late.
- A caller who's been given a wrong answer before: the ground has a
  hole in it already, and the first post goes wherever that hole is.

**Reading which order this particular call needs is the skill.** Not
"what is the answer" — where does this one need the first post.

**The posts are not the same list as the five phases** (see "Ticket
Resolution — Investigation Phase"). The phases are *surfaces the
interface offers* — Identify, Classify, Investigate, Diagnose,
Disposition. The posts are *what a good call is made of*. They cut
across each other: Empathy is not a surface the player visits, it is
something present or absent while they are at any of them, and
Education most often happens during Diagnose. Both lists are five long
and both are non-linear, which makes them easy to confuse; they are
different axes. See "Open Questions" — how they wire together is not
settled. This
is what replaces a knowledge base (see "Running the Desk," which
establishes that TLC has none): the expertise is structural and
diagnostic, not encyclopedic.

**Gating is not a rule in the system — it's a property of the
ground.** Support genuinely can't precede Investigation, because
there's nothing to reason from yet. But Empathy-before-everything
isn't a law; it's what this caller, today, requires. A different
caller doesn't need it first. Encoding "Empathy always gates" as a
mechanical rule would reproduce exactly the rigidity the four
universal response buttons already have, five times over.

### How this recontextualizes what's already built

Mapping the current prototype onto the five posts is the clearest
statement of what's wrong with it:

- **Request / Requestor** — one line of opening text. The *Requestor*
  half doesn't exist at all: every caller is mechanically identical
  until the Character Disposition Model is built.
- **Investigation** — built, for one ticket category out of ten (see
  "Ticket Resolution — Investigation Phase," which is one post of
  five, not the whole call).
- **Support** — the "diagnosis" step: pick one of four pre-written
  conclusions. No hypothesis, no elimination, no scientific method.
- **Education** — absent.
- **Sympathy / Empathy** — absent.

The four universal response buttons collapse all five posts into a
**single quality scalar**. That is the root defect, stated precisely:
five things happen on a call and the sim models one of them.

Note that **the two absent posts are the two that are pure human
judgment** — the two that need no reference material and no lookup.
The build has the machine parts and not the person parts.

**This also solves the content problem.** The five posts are universal;
only their content varies per call. That's one template, not ten
bespoke designs — "after a while all the calls are the same" is a
production strategy as much as an observation.

**And it kills "the correct answer is pre-labeled" without a knowledge
base.** "Walk them through the full fix" is strong on Support and can
be actively wrong on Empathy, on a caller who needed to be heard
first. Same option, different call, different outcome — judgment about
the person, not retrieval of a fact.

### The case note is showing your work

**Once the fence is built, the case note is as simple as showing your
work in math class.**

The note is not a separate task performed after the call. It is the
**artifact** of the call — the fence, described. Nothing has to be
composed, because the structure of the note is the structure of the
work that was just done. A call with all five posts set produces its
note nearly for free. **A call missing posts can't produce a good note
at any price**, because there's nothing to show. You cannot write up
an investigation you didn't perform.

*(This supersedes an earlier framing in "Running the Desk," which
treated wrap-up as a separate cost the player pays after each call —
rush it, get a thinner file. That was backwards: it imported after-call
work as a chore, which is what it becomes in a badly-run center, not
what it is when the job is done properly. There is no wrap-up
minigame and no time budget for note-taking. What lands in the record
is just what actually happened.)*

### But people lie

**The note is a claim about the fence, not a readout of it.** It's
written by a person, and people lie — or pad, or flatter themselves,
or honestly believe they educated a customer they didn't. An unset
post therefore does **not** automatically appear as a hole in the
record. It appears as a hole only if the note is honest, or if someone
checks.

This is why quality review has to mean *reviewing the call*, not
reading the writeup and ticking boxes. It's also why "Trust and
Verify" exists as frontline wisdom at all: **Investigation is a
separate post from Request precisely because the Request can't be
taken at face value.** Callers shade things too — not usually
maliciously, but a request is always an account, and accounts are
partial.

So there are now three imperfect records of the same call, and they
disagree for three different and honest reasons:
1. **What actually happened.**
2. **What the customer remembers** — decays and distorts over shifts
   (`recallMemory()`, built).
3. **What the note says** — as complete as the fence that was built,
   and as truthful as the person who wrote it.

The game's stated core loop is imperfect customer memory shaping
future encounters. This is the other half of it: an imperfect
*company* memory, shaped by the player.

### Reading your own file back

**The player reads their own notes, and that's the point.** Not
retrieval — **re-evaluation.**

A note is a record of a past self's understanding. The present self
knows more: three shifts of pattern recognition later, they may now
know the answer they didn't have then. So the interesting encounter
isn't "a returning customer with a disposition score." It's **a
customer whose file you wrote, which you can now see was wrong, while
they're still carrying the wrong answer you gave them.**

That's a repair call, and it's the most valuable scenario this whole
structure produces:
- The caller remembers being told something (possibly misremembering
  it — see `recallMemory()`).
- The note says what the player claimed at the time.
- The player now knows better.
- The call is about **fixing it** — which is Empathy, Education, and
  Support all at once, on a foundation the player themselves poured
  badly.

This is cozy in the way that matters: the consequence of an early
mistake is not a penalty, it's *a chance to go back and set it right*,
with a person who remembers. It also gives the "History doesn't repeat
itself, it can rhyme though" player-learning dimension something
concrete to bite on — player skill accumulates, but old notes don't
update themselves.

*Open, not guessed at*: whether the player can choose to write a
dishonest note (and what that costs them later when they're the one
reading it); whether notes can be amended once the truth is known, or
whether the original stands as written; and whether correcting a wrong
answer clears the customer's memory of it or just adds to it. See
"Open Questions."

Not built — design only.

## Ticket Resolution — Investigation Phase

**Replaces the current model, which doesn't work — locked.** The
existing single-step flow (a `blurb` that already states the
diagnosis, then one pick from four universal, content-free quality
buttons) fails two ways at once: there's no puzzle, because the
answer is handed over before the player does anything; and there's no
meaningful choice either, because the four options aren't connected
to any specific ticket's content. A player could click "Walk them
through the full fix" on every ticket forever and win every time,
with zero comprehension required. Confirmed directly against the
actual "Phantom Inventory" ticket: its blurb — *"Storefront says 40
potions in stock. Shelf says 3."* — already **is** the diagnosis, not
a clue toward one.

**The new structure, five phases — and they are NOT linear:**

1. **Identify** — who is actually on the line, and what they want. The
   player picks up **blind**; the registry offers a channel and an
   unmatched ward signature and nothing else.

   **The greeting is the universal opening move, and it is a move the
   player has to make.** "TLC Support, how may I help you?" is the
   question that prompts the customer to give their reason for calling.
   Nothing about the caller — not their reason, not their tier, not
   their archetype — should be handed to the player before they open
   the call. A ticket that displays its own complaint the moment it
   appears has skipped the first thing that happens on every call in
   the world.

   **Greeting them does not reliably get you their reason for calling.**
   On a real desk you are lucky to get it that cleanly. People lead with
   a past problem, with how long they were kept holding, with the
   product or service in general, with something vague, or with feeling
   before fact. Getting to the actual request is work, and it costs
   time. Roughly a quarter of calls open with a clean statement of the
   problem; the rest have to be worked toward.

   **Nobody shares every detail at first, and Baggage drives the manner
   of the telling.** What a customer walked in already carrying decides
   whether they are terse, defensive, circling, over-sharing, or
   forthcoming — so the *way* information arrives is characterisation,
   not just a gate in front of content. A guarded caller volunteers the
   request less often and gives less when they do. (See "Character
   Disposition Model." The hook for this exists in `rollOpening()` and
   is deliberately inert until Phase 1 gives Baggage real values.)

   **Cozy bound, locked**: hard to get to the point, never a dead end.
   Pressing a second time always lands. A caller can cost the player
   time; they can never strand them.

   The customer's answer is their own perception, partial and sometimes
   wrong, and **never the diagnosis**. Example, reworked: *"I ordered 40 healing potions for
   the festival, and now you're telling me I don't have them?! Where
   did they go?"* Could be a lost shipment, a Wildline transformation
   mishap, a stockroom problem, or a system miscount — nothing there
   says which.
2. **Classify** — what kind of problem this is, filed as a term of
   art (see "Naming Methodology" on category names). **Provisional by
   nature**: filed on partial information and revised as evidence
   comes in. Being wrong here and correcting it later is ordinary
   work, not a failure — and being wrong and *never* correcting it is
   how a ticket gets reclassified by somebody else after the fact.
   This is also what makes "Pigs is Pigs" (see "Screening &
   Classification") a live tension on every call rather than a
   special ticket type.
3. **Investigate** — the player picks from a menu of concrete
   actions, each costing time, each revealing one real clue (check
   the shipment manifest, check the Node's delivery log, ask about
   stockroom access, check for recent bulk activity). Time cost makes
   investigating everything a real tradeoff against the shift clock,
   not a formality.
4. **Diagnose** — ticket-specific, correct only if grounded in what
   was actually uncovered. A plausible-sounding wrong answer should
   be available if the player didn't dig past a red herring.
5. **Disposition** (resolved/hold/transfer) — as already built.

**Non-linearity is locked, and it is the whole point.** These five are
**not a sequence, not a corridor, and not gated by position.** Real
progress on a call bounces — back, forward, sideways, diagonally. A
classification gets revised three questions in. An answer the caller
gives mid-investigation turns out to be identifying information. A
hypothesis forms, gets tested, and gets discarded, sending the player
back to ask something else. The player may need to re-identify
partway through because the person on the line turns out not to be
the account's named contact at all.

**Implementation rule that follows**: the five are **surfaces that
stay available**, not states that close behind the player. Gate on
*evidence* where the fiction demands it — a diagnosis the player
cannot back up should be unavailable, and that is correct — but never
gate on *sequence position*. "You cannot classify because you have
not identified yet" is a corridor, not a call: an agent frequently
knows what kind of problem they are looking at well before they know
who they are talking to.

**This is the same shape as the five fenceposts** (see "The Five
Fenceposts — What Every Call Is Made Of") and for the same reason. The
posts are a layout, not a sequence; the phases are surfaces, not
steps. Anything that reduces either one to a fixed order reintroduces
exactly the rigidity that made the original four universal response
buttons worthless. The failure mode is *extremely* easy to fall back
into — both the first design draft of this and a review of it defaulted
to a linear reading within a day of the non-linearity being written
down — which is why it is stated this loudly here.

*(Supersedes the earlier four-step formulation — "opening statement →
investigation → diagnosis → status" — which both collapsed Identify
into flavor text and omitted Classify entirely. Five phases preferred,
per Phil. The old four-step wording should not be restored.)*

### The game does not grade the diagnosis — others do. Locked.

**The agent's focus is the act of finding a solution, not arriving at an
answer the game already holds.** A diagnosis can be **correct, partial,
or wrong**, and that is not the agent's determination to make. **Others
judge the work** — the customer, coworkers, the Co-Op — and **the
results of that judgment are what trigger the agent finding out more.**

This is a correction to how both the built code and the design handoff
frame the diagnose phase. Both treat the ticket as a puzzle with one
answer the game knows, checked instantly:

- The design keys every ending off `(dx === 'sync' ? 'sync' : 'wrong')`
  — a **binary**, with no room for partial at all — and colours the
  diagnosis green or red the moment it is picked.
- The built `resolveInvestigation()` is closer, returning four grades
  rather than two, but still adjudicates on the spot.

**What should happen instead**: the player concludes something and
*acts* on it. The world responds on its own timetable. A wrong call
comes back as a dispute; a partial one comes back half-solved or as a
second contact about the same thing; a coworker mentions the thing you
missed; the Co-Op reclassifies it over your head. **That reaction is the
grade**, and it arrives later, through somebody, exactly like the survey
chain in "Running the Desk."

**Partial is the missing middle and probably the most common real
outcome.** You identified the right kind of problem and the wrong
instance of it. You fixed what they called about and missed what it was
a symptom of. Neither "right" nor "wrong" describes that, and a binary
forces every such call into one bucket or the other.

**This is the knowledge half of the roguelite growth loop.** Skills
unlock through coaching and training (see "Education & Empathy"); *what
the player knows about the work* grows a different way — you get one
wrong, it comes back, and the coming-back is what teaches you. That loop
does not exist if the game says ❌ at the moment of choosing.

**Consequence for gating.** The design gates the correct diagnosis
behind having found two specific clues — the option is unclickable until
then. That enforces correctness rather than letting a wrong answer play
out, which is the opposite of this rule. The built code's approach is
better aligned: it lets the player say it, and an ungrounded-but-correct
guess simply doesn't land as well as a grounded one. Prefer *the world
responds differently* over *the option is disabled*.

**Two structural rules, locked:**

- **How many investigative actions are available, and how many the
  player can afford to pick, varies per ticket — not tied to
  urgency.** A simple-sounding request can turn out complex; a
  request that reads as complex can turn out simpler than it seemed.
  If urgency reliably predicted investigation depth, reading a ticket
  correctly would require no skill — a player could triage by the
  urgency number alone. Decoupling them is what makes "History
  Rhymes" (Customer Service Philosophy) mean something at the
  ticket-content level, not just the customer-memory level.
- **A genuinely misleading clue is present on *some* tickets, not
  all.** If every ticket had a trap, the only sane strategy would be
  reflexive distrust of everything — which isn't "Trust and Verify,"
  it's just "verify." The principle only holds if trusting the
  customer's account is usually the right call.

`resolveInvestigation()`/`investigationTimeCost()` functions in
`gameLogic.js`, 10 new tests including one that directly proves the
old failure mode is gone (under-investigating and fully investigating
now produce different, tested outcomes), and a full headless-browser
click-through of the actual JSX prototype (mount → find a Phantom
Inventory ticket → investigate the key clue → diagnose correctly →
confirm it advances) — genuinely played, not just unit-tested.

**The JSX prototype is behind this section, and it should be said
plainly.** Phase 0 built the investigate-then-diagnose flow for one
ticket category; the other nine still use the old flat four-button
model. The five phases exist in this document and in the design team's
handoff, not in `CallCenterPrototype.jsx`. Two consequences visible in
play right now: there is **no greeting** — a ticket prints its own
complaint and the caller's archetype the instant it appears, so the
player never picks up blind and never asks — and there is no Classify
step at all. The design handoff gets both right and our prototype does
not.

**A real, honest residual risk found during that verification, not
fully solved**: diagnosis options are shown immediately alongside the
investigation actions, not gated behind them. The correct option's own
label ("It's a system miscount — the festival sale wasn't logged")
contains enough of the answer that a player could, in principle, read
all four labels cold and blind-guess the most specific-sounding one
without investigating anything. The mechanic already blunts this — a
correct guess without the key clue caps at `adequate`, never `correct`
(tested) — but it's a softer version of the original spoiler problem,
not a full fix. Left as an open question below rather than silently
declared solved.

## Ticket Content — Categories, Sub-Variables, and the Telling

**The problem: ten categories is not a game.** Hand-authoring each
ticket type produces content that runs out. Ten categories means ten
calls, and after that the player has seen everything and is choosing
from a menu they have memorised.

**Locked: a category is a template, not a script.** Three layers stack
on top of each other, and the combinations are where volume comes from
without hand-writing every case.

### 3b. Register — customer type shapes the telling too

**Baggage decides how willingly someone tells you; customer type decides
how they sound doing it.** A Military account and a Wandering Trader
with identical traits, identical Baggage and the identical complaint
will not say it the same way.

Five registers, keyed to archetype:

| Register | Who | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **formal** | Kingdom, Town Council, Constabulary, Military | procedural, institutional |
| **grand** | Hero, Villain | theatrical; the call is about them |
| **plain** | NPC, Blacksmith, Henchfolk | few words, no performance |
| **chatty** | Tavern, Inn, Wandering Trader | discursive, gets there eventually |
| **deferential** | Sidekick, Potion Shop | hedging, reluctant to take up your time |

**This does not violate the archetype constraint.** LORE.md forbids
archetype as a formula input for Baggage, Sensitivity or Social Filter —
and it still is; `rollTraits()` does not take a customer at all, which is
the structural guarantee. **Register is expression; the traits are
substance.** The two layer.

### 3c. Familiarity — a regular talks like someone who knows the place

A third dimension, independent of both register and Baggage: **how well
this caller knows TLC.**

A stranger states a grievance flat. **A regular in good standing hands
you an excuse unprompted** — *"I've been on hold quite a while, must be
busy today"* — because they have goodwill and no interest in spending it
here. A regular in bad standing does the reverse, placing today's call
in a pattern, because for them there is one.

This **cuts across archetype**: a Kingdom and a Tavern who both know you
both soften. Register only decides the words they soften in.

**Written as a coda, not as new lines.** Familiarity composes on top of
the register's wording rather than multiplying it — otherwise every mode
× register × familiarity is sixty variations nobody would write well.

**But a coda must be in the speaker's voice.** A grand caller who has
just demanded *"do you know who I am?"* does not then say *"must be busy
today"* — they are magnanimous instead: *"I will allow that you are
busy."* Same intent, different person. Codas therefore override per
register where the default would contradict the speaker.

*Writing rule, learned the hard way*: **do not let a line undercut
itself.** A rejected draft read *"I don't want to make a fuss. I have
been holding rather a while, though"* — a character announcing they will
not do the thing and then doing it. That is a writer's tic, not speech.
If a line contains its own contradiction, the character is being written
rather than heard.

*Known gap*: register currently varies the OPENING lines only. The
request itself — the category blurb — is still one wording for everyone.

### 0. The domain — the real taxonomy, locked

Above categories sit the **nine problem domains a contact centre
actually handles**:

> **Orders · Fulfillment · Quality · Quantity · Returns · Repairs ·
> Service · Accounts · Billing**

This is the real-world taxonomy, and it replaces an earlier ad-hoc
arrangement in which flavour categories were bucketed by **customer
tier** — which was arbitrary. **Domains cut across tiers.** A Kingdom,
a Tavern and a Hero can all have a Billing problem, and organising by
who is calling hid that.

Full structure, largest to smallest:

> **domain** (9, fixed) → **category** (a term of art) → **root cause**
> → told through **perspective** and **Baggage**

**The domains are the frame, not slots to be filled.** They are broad
and stable; categories are the narrow, contextual things written inside
them. An earlier pass got this backwards — scoring domains by how many
existing categories happened to land in each, and reading "Orders has
none" as a hole. It is not a hole. The old categories were written
before the frame existed, so where they fall says nothing about the
frame's health; it only says where writing has happened so far.

Where things currently sit:

| Domain | Categories written |
| --- | --- |
| Orders | **none** |
| Fulfillment | Bulk Potion Order Delay, Garrison Supply Shortfall |
| Quality | Quest Line Bug |
| Quantity | Phantom Inventory |
| Returns | Cursed Item Return |
| Repairs | Border Ward Malfunction |
| Service | Respawn Delay |
| Accounts | XP Miscount Dispute, Storefront Listing Error |
| Billing | Commission Payout Dispute |

Read that as a map of where writing has happened, not a scorecard. New
categories get written *into* the frame as the game needs them, and a
domain sitting empty is simply one nothing has called for yet.

A root cause carries a `trueDomain` as well as a `trueCategory`, because
misclassification happens at both levels. Phantom Inventory's seven
causes land across **four** domains: Quantity, Fulfillment, Accounts,
and Service.

### 0b. Basic requests — the bread and butter of each domain

The ordinary calls. Not exotica: these are what actually comes in, day
after day, and a support centre is mostly made of them.

**Names are terms of art**, per "Naming Methodology" — what the trade
would call the problem type, recognisable to a customer as *their*
situation without being the sentence they would say. Existing built
categories are marked **[built]**.

**Shape** says which of the call's machinery a request leans on:
*records* (answerable from our own paperwork), *ask* (needs what only
they know), *educate* (the work is explaining), *boundary* (frequently
turns out not to be ours), *divergence* (our record and their memory
tend to disagree).

#### Orders — placing, changing, cancelling

| Request | What they experience | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Booking Request | wants something sent, needs it arranged | educate |
| Booking Change | destination, count or tier wrong before dispatch | records |
| Booking Cancellation | wants to stop something already booked | records |
| Tier Quote | which service do I need, what will it cost | educate |
| Standing Order Setup | recurring shipments, usually Caravan | educate |
| No Booking On Record | certain they ordered; we have nothing | divergence, boundary |

#### Fulfillment — getting it there

| Request | What they experience | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |

| Late Arrival | past the window they were given | records, educate |

| Failed Handover | nobody there to sign, or the Node was shut | records, ask |
| Redirect Request | change the destination mid-route | educate |
| Split Delivery | arrived in parts; they expected one | records, educate |

#### Quality — it arrived wrong or broken

| Request | What they experience | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |

| Spoilage | potions turned, perishables degraded | ask |
| Wrong Goods | not what was sent, or not what was ordered | records, divergence |
| Seal Breach | opened or tampered with before arrival | records, ask |

#### Quantity — the count disagrees

| Request | What they experience | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |

| **Phantom Inventory** **[built]** | the listing and the shelf disagree | records, ask, boundary |
| Overage | more arrived than ordered | records |

| --- | --- | --- |

#### Billing — money

| Request | What they experience | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overcharge Dispute | charged more than expected | records, boundary |
| Duplicate Charge | charged twice for one thing | records |

| Upcharge Dispute | a size or weight upcharge nobody authorised | records, boundary |
| Invoice Query | what is this line for | educate |

**Phantom Inventory is a subcategory, not a top-level thing.** It is one
specific shape of a count going wrong — the listing says one number and
the shelf says another — and its causes already span four domains. Most
of the requests above are broader and more ordinary than it.

### 1. The category

The classification — a term of art for a real problem type, per
"Naming Methodology." "Phantom Inventory." This is what gets *filed*,
and it is the smallest of the three layers.

### 2. Sub-variables

The specifics that vary per instance. What was shipped, how much, to
where, against what deadline, and — critically — **what actually went
wrong.** The same category can have genuinely different root causes:
Phantom Inventory can be an unlogged sale, a duplicated courier stamp,
a stockroom problem, or a Node that never posted a receipt. The
investigation actions and their clues follow from the root cause, so
one category yields many distinct puzzles rather than one puzzle with
new nouns.

**A sub-variable is only worth having if it changes what the right
answer is, or how you would find it.** Swapping potions for ingots is
set dressing; swapping the root cause is content.

### 3. The telling — perspective and Baggage

**The same facts, expressed differently, are a different call.** Two
layers distort the telling before it reaches the player:

- **Perspective** — what the customer *believes* happened, which is
  partial and often wrong. They are describing their experience, not
  filing a report. The opening statement is never the diagnosis (see
  "Ticket Resolution").
- **Baggage** — what they walked in carrying decides *how* it comes
  out: terse, defensive, circling back, over-explaining, apologetic,
  furious, or flatly matter-of-fact. Baggage is not just a number that
  moves; it is the manner of the telling. See "Character Disposition
  Model."

This is the layer that makes a category feel inexhaustible. A blacksmith
who has been burned before and a first-time tavern keeper report the
*same* root cause completely differently, and the player's job — reading
which is which — is the same skill on both.

**Consequence for content work**: authoring a new category means
authoring a root-cause set and its clues, not a single scripted call.
Expression is generated from traits, not written per case.

### What TLC can actually see — locked

**TLC's visibility ends where the partnership agreement ends — not at
the loading dock.** A Node is a formal role in TLC's own network, and so
is a Courier. **Both report.** A Courier reports the delivery: when,
where, and to whom. A Node reports receipt. That is not a courtesy, it
is the arrangement — a business does not get to be a waypoint in the
network and also be a black box. So TLC knows perfectly well what it
shipped, where it went, who took it, and that the Node acknowledged it.

**Who signs, and where — it is not complicated.** A **Node** is a
*place*: the shipment lands there and **the customer collects and
signs**. A **Courier** is an *actor*: they carry it and **the customer
signs** on delivery. Which leg is last decides which happens. Either
way it is the customer's signature.

So a signature is always meaningful, and a name on a receipt that means
nothing to the caller is a real problem in both cases.

**But TLC only holds the name.** Whether that person works there is the
customer's knowledge, not ours — which means it does not exist in our
records at all. It comes into being when the agent reads the name out
and gets contradicted:

> *"It was signed for by Jimothy, on the 11th."*
> *"Jimothy? We've no Jimothy — nor a Timothy, for that matter."*

**Sharing a finding is therefore a move that can return information**,
and one of the most productive an agent makes. Support and investigation
are not separable stages: telling somebody what you hold is frequently
how the next thread appears.

**A missing report is itself information**, and a partnership problem in
its own right. A Node that never posted receipt has told you something
by not telling you anything.

What TLC does **not** see is everything downstream of that receipt:
what happened to the goods once they were inside somebody else's
operation. **It does not know what is on a customer's shelf**, and an
agent on a ley-line cannot walk anyone's stockroom.

**POOF is instant delivery. It is not instant unpacking, shelving, or
bookkeeping.** A shipment arriving in a blink still has to be received
by one person, shelved by another, and entered into the merchant's own
records by a third — and after that, stock leaves through sales,
returns, damage, and staff. **Every one of those hands is a place where
the shelf and the ledger can drift apart, and none of them belong to
TLC.** That gap is where most "phantom inventory" actually lives.

It gets wider still, because a merchant's stock does not all come from
TLC. They take **returns**, and they **buy inventory outright from
adventurers** hauling loot in. A shelf holds goods TLC shipped, goods
TLC never touched, and goods that came back — mixed together under one
count. TLC's records cannot reconcile a number they only ever saw part
of.

**Mechanical consequence — investigation splits in two:**

- **Records** — things the agent can check directly, because the
  network reports them: what was shipped, the Courier's delivery report,
  the Node's receipt, a Caravan draw against the lot, a hold placed on
  goods in TLC's custody. Fast and reliable, and **limited to what the
  agreement covers.**
- **Asks** — things only the customer can find out: counting the shelf,
  who received and who shelved it, their own sales ledger, what else
  came in that week. **Slower, costly to their patience, and only as
  good as their own bookkeeping.** Baggage shapes how willingly and how
  accurately they answer.

### An agent asks what you know. An agent does not set you homework.

**Locked, after two builds got it wrong.** "Go and count your shelf" and
"go and check your own sales ledger" were both on the menu as things an
agent could do. Both are TLC **dispatching a customer to carry out TLC's
investigation**, inside a business we cannot see, about records that are
not ours. Making them merely *expensive* was not the fix; they should
never have been options.

The line: **asking what somebody knows from where they are standing is a
question. Sending them off to do work is not.** "Who normally takes
deliveries in?" is a question. "Go and audit your books and call us
back" is homework, and it is also an accusation.

Conversational questions are cheap — a single inquiry — because that is
what they cost in life.

### Three endings, and none of them require a named cause — locked

This was already established and a later model contradicted it, so it is
restated flatly:

- **Resolve** — the agent **believes** the request was given an
  appropriate solution. A belief, not a verified truth. It sticks, or it
  is disputed later by somebody else.
- **Hold** — action pending: on the **agent**, on the **customer**, on
  an **internal** process, or **more than one at once**.
- **Transfer** — the call or case is handed off for further review.

**A diagnosis is one route to Resolve. It is not the only one and it is
not required.** Confirming our part is clean and handing over the detail
resolves a call perfectly well without naming anything at all.

The model that went wrong assumed every call must end with a named
cause, which is precisely what kept pushing the agent into the
customer's own books looking for one. The three endings already said
otherwise.

*(An earlier build also flattened Hold to internal/external, which lost
the agent's own pending work entirely and lost the fact that holds
**stack** — a case waiting on the customer AND on an internal process is
ordinary, not exceptional.)*

### When our part is discharged, saying so IS the answer

**Not every root cause is TLC's to find.** Our sight ends at the Node's
receipt. When the records are clean — shipped, delivered, signed, receipt
posted — the correct determination is *"everything on our side checks
out, here is the delivery detail"*, at **full standing**. It is not a
partial answer, and it is not a failure to diagnose.

What happened afterwards, inside their operation, is genuinely theirs.
An agent who keeps going past this point is not being thorough; they are
rummaging, and that is where both the blame and the condescension in
earlier drafts came from. **You can only tell somebody they failed to
log a sale if you went looking in their books first.**

So a cause is marked `discoverable` or not:

| Discoverable from our records | Not ours to find |
| --- | --- |
| Split lot and Caravan draw | Unlogged sale |
| Second leg still in transit | Received and shelved but never entered |
| Constabulary hold | Misfiled at intake — unless *they* volunteer it |
| Counter-bought (we shipped nothing) | |

**The undiscoverable ones still have a right answer.** It is just not the
cause — it is the discharge, delivered cleanly, with the detail handed
over. Guessing at their internals from a clean record scores as
overreach, not insight.

*(And a cause that is invisible to a lookup can still come out — when the
agent shares what they hold and the caller contradicts it. "Shipped as
Small." "Small? We shelved those as Large." We could never have found
that; they could never have known to say it.)*

### "Go and check your shelves" — the timing is the service

**An ask is never neutral.** Sending a customer off to count their own
stock puts the work back on them and carries an unspoken accusation: *I
think you have miscounted, and I have not looked myself.*

**What decides how it lands is whether the agent has visibly done their
own homework first.** These are the same request:

> *"Go and count your shelf."*
>
> *"Everything on our side checks out — the manifest, the Courier's
> report, the Node's receipt. Can I ask you to do one thing for me?"*

The first is an accusation. The second is collaboration, and it works
because the customer can hear that the agent already spent their own
effort before spending theirs.

**Locked**: an ask made before any record has been checked is
**unearned** and costs the relationship — it lands on the **You** axis,
because it is a fact about the agent, not the company. An ask made after
costs only Experience, because it is still their time and trouble.
Repeated cold asks compound. **Order matters, not just which actions
were taken.**

Empathy would soften an unearned ask, but Empathy is an earned skill and
is not built (see "Education & Empathy") — a new agent genuinely does
not know how to make this land, which is exactly right for the early
game.

**Some calls cannot be solved from TLC's records at all.** The agent's
reach is genuinely limited, and getting the customer to do the legwork
is part of the job — not a fallback. An agent who only ever checks
their own systems will close a whole class of ticket wrongly.

*(Two earlier builds got this wrong in opposite directions. The first
included "walk the shelf with them, crate by crate" — an action no call
centre agent can perform. The correction then overshot, implying TLC
ships goods and never learns who received them or where. Both are wrong:
Nodes and Couriers report because their agreements require it. The line
is the agreement, not the dock.)*

### The case note — composed, not logged

**Captured through inquiries, processed by the agent's knowledge,
rendered as a case note.** Three separate things. An early draft
collapsed them and produced a transcript, which no agent writes.

**Three sections, and they are the three aspects of any support
interaction:**

- **Reason for contact** — what they came to you with, in their terms.
  Not what you concluded. Conflating the two makes the note assign fault
  by construction.
- **Support provided** — what was checked, what came back, and **who
  found it**.
- **Next steps** — what happens now. When TLC has none, do not invent
  one for the customer to be responsible for.

**Frames are keyed to the DOMAIN** the player filed it under, with the
category supplying slot vocabulary. Twenty-seven frames rather than
three per category, and a new category inherits a note shape for free.
*This may move to category level later* if a Billing note and a
Commission Payout note turn out to want genuinely different frames —
recorded so the decision is known to be provisional rather than
discovered again.

**Written in a frame chosen by the classification the player made.** File
it wrong and you write the note in the wrong shape entirely — a note
about commission when the problem was stock. The misclassification
propagates into the record, visibly, for whoever reads next.

**Slots fill from what came up in the call**, not from what was
established — so the red herring sits in the list beside the answer, and
no distractors need authoring. The call already produced them.

**Blanks are allowed, but capped at one or two.** A gap is the honest
artifact of a thin call and is far better than an invention, because the
next reader can see a gap. Past the cap the player must put *something*,
which produces wrong notes rather than empty ones — and a wrong note
misleads later, which is the more interesting failure.

**Composed after the call, from recall.** *Memory is a fleeting thing.*
This is what makes the anchoring rule bite: a detail that was acted on
survives sharp, a detail heard once and never used goes fuzzy or goes
missing. The agent's memory is imperfect for the same reason the
customer's is — both were there, neither was taking dictation.

**Two things the note must never do**, both learned from drafts that did
them:

1. **Assign blame.** *"Their own sale went out unentered"*, *"caller to
   enter sales as they go"*, *"nothing outstanding our end"* — three
   sentences, three separate accusations, and the next agent now
   approaches that customer as someone who cannot keep books. Often
   nobody is at fault, and a good note does not need the question
   answered.
2. **Claim knowledge TLC does not have.** *"Located a festival sale"* —
   located how? That is their ledger, in their shop. Every finding must
   be attributed to its source, and the data already knows which:
   `records` findings are ours, `ask` findings are theirs. Built that
   way, omniscience is unwriteable.

### The shape of a call — one Co-Op inquiry, nine of the player's

**Locked: a call is 1 + 9.**

The **opening is the Co-Op's, not the player's**. "Thank you for calling
TLC, how may I help you?" is a requirement of the job, said on the
company's behalf. The player gets no credit for saying the script and no
blame either, so it costs them nothing.

After that the player has **nine inquiries** to work with. Twenty is far
too many — nobody sustains twenty questions and no caller would tolerate
it. Ten sits wrong; it reads as a round number rather than a shape. Nine
leaves the opening standing apart as what it actually is.

*(That there are also nine problem domains is a happy coincidence, not a
design. Do not build anything that maps one to the other.)*

**Two clocks, doing different jobs:**

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **The candle** | the shift. One notch per call, untouched by anything inside a call. Not a budget. |
| **The nine** | this call's natural length. Resets every call, so spending it is never hoarding. |

**Running out is not failure.** At zero the agent makes a determination
on what they have — under-informed, and adjudicated later by other
people like every other determination.

### An inquiry is an intent, not a sentence — locked

**Inquiries are not always questions.** These all obtain the same thing:

> *"May I have your name?"*
> *"And what is your name?"*
> *"To proceed, please provide your name."*
> *"The next thing we need is your name."*
> *"I'm Wren, and your name?"*
> *"Thank you for that. To continue we need your name."*

Same intent. Six **deliveries**, and three of them are not questions at
all. The information obtained is identical. **What changes is how it
lands** — and that depends on who is on the line and on the agent
delivering it.

So an inquiry has two parts, and the player chooses both:

- **The intent** — what you are trying to obtain. This is what spends
  one of the nine.
- **The delivery** — how you put it. Free, and most of the actual job.

Deliveries vary on three axes: **directness** (how hard it presses),
**warmth** (how much of the agent is in it), and **formality**
(register). A directive to somebody already upset is cold. A reciprocal
*"I'm Wren, and your name?"* to a Kingdom account is over-familiar.
**Neither is wrong in the abstract** — and nothing is universally right,
which there is a test for. If one phrasing always won, the player would
learn it once and stop thinking.

What a caller wants is set by their register (the formality they are
comfortable with) plus their current state: somebody **upset** wants
warmth, somebody **worn down by the episode** wants you to get to the
point. Those are different needs and must not collapse into one.

Worked example — the same request to an upset, chatty Tavern:

| Fit | Delivery |
| --- | --- |
| **0.71** | *"I'm Wren, and your name?"* |
| 0.57 | *"And your name?"* |
| 0.43 | *"May I have your name?"* |
| 0.29 | *"To proceed, please provide your name."* |
| **0.14** | *"The next thing we need is your name."* |

To a calm Kingdom account the order roughly inverts, and the
transactional form scores best of all.

### What a caller wants is a continuum, and it moves — locked

**Do not derive what somebody wants from their state.** Two drafts got
this wrong in escalating ways, and both are worth recording because the
pull toward them is strong:

1. *"Upset means they want warmth."* A lookup table wearing a formula.
   The player learns it once and stops thinking.
2. *"Some want soothing, some want you to get on with it."* Better —
   and still **two camps with the middle thrown away.** Most people live
   in that middle.

**It is a continuum**, and where a caller sits on it is a fact about
*that character at that time* — not about being upset.

**Nobody wants identical service every time.** What somebody wants is
tailored to **what they are feeling**, **what is happening to them right
now**, and **what happened to them before**. Service is not a setting
you find once per customer and reuse.

Four things stack, all continuous:

- **Their own baseline.** Individually rolled, exactly like Baggage and
  Sensitivity. Register *biases* the formality they are comfortable
  with; it never sets it, and plenty of formal speakers want warmth.
- **Their own direction under strain.** A signed vector rolled per
  person. Some move toward warmth as a call goes badly, some away, and
  **some barely move at all** — not everyone changes under pressure.
- **What is happening to them right now.** Urgency is what is at stake
  this minute; a follow-up means we have already failed them once, and a
  **reopened** ticket means we failed them *after telling them it was
  fixed*, which presses hardest of all. This is the piece an earlier
  draft was missing entirely — it had a random per-call jitter standing
  in for situation, which is a dice roll wearing a story.
- **Today.** What is left over once feeling and situation are accounted
  for — the ordinary variation of a person having a day. *"Even the most
  loyal, best-known customer isn't identical day to day."* It nudges; it
  is deliberately the smallest of the four, and it is **not** a
  substitute for modelling what is actually happening to someone.

**Every source supplies magnitude only. The direction is always the
individual's.** That single rule is what stops each of these becoming a
lookup table in turn: no amount of urgency, no depth of grievance, and
no repeated failure ever makes two people want the same handling. There
is a test that puts 300 different people in an identical crisis and
fails if they converge.

Measured across 2,000 equally-upset callers, wanted warmth forms a
single smooth hump centred near neutral, tailing off to both extremes
with **no gap between the middle and the poles**. That shape is the
requirement: a bimodal result would mean the two camps had crept back
in.

**This is where Empathy actually lives.** Choosing the delivery that
suits this person *is* the skill. Empathy is earned (see "Education &
Empathy"), so a new agent picks by instinct and finds out afterwards —
and finds out only through the customer's cues, which are ambiguous by
design. The game never scores the choice.

*Implementation note*: phrasings are written once as forms with a slot
for the intent's subject — "your name", "the shelf count", "who signed
for it" — so one set of deliveries serves every inquiry rather than six
phrasings being written per action.

### Three kinds of inquiry — a tendency, not a sequence

A rough shape of how the nine tend to fall, **explicitly not locked**:

- **2–3 investigative** — establishing the reason for contact
- **2–3 supportive** — the support actually provided
- **2–3 wrap-up** — next steps: time frames, what they are waiting on,
  what they must do themselves

**This is a distribution, not a structure.** Every customer and scenario
wants a different chain, and **customer support is not a linear
process**: an inquiry of one kind routinely triggers a follow-up of
another as information arrives. You are three questions into wrapping up,
they mention something in passing, and you are investigating again.
Nothing may encode a stage, a prerequisite, or an order — there is a
test that fails if any action grows a `stage`, `step`, `requires` or
`unlocks` field.

**What this exposed**: an earlier build had *only* investigative
actions. The player could find out what was wrong and had no way to **do
anything about it**, and no way to tell the customer **what happens
next**. Both are now real inquiries with their own costs.

Wrap-up is also where **Education** naturally lives — explaining in
plain terms how a thing happened is the wrap-up inquiry and the
Education fencepost at the same time.

**Support is judged, not free.** Every supportive action is available on
every call, and most are the wrong thing on any given one. Correcting a
listing fixes a misfiled lot and does nothing at all for goods under a
Constabulary hold. The menu never disables anything; it simply lets you
be wrong.

### Reading a customer — cues, never a verdict. Locked.

**A label is not how anyone learns what a caller feels.** On a real desk
you get **tone, word choice, phrasing, volume, pitch and cadence**, and
you work it out. Every one of those is ambiguous on its own and none of
them is a readout.

So the game never states how a customer feels. `readCues()` returns
**observable behaviour** — the cadence of their answers, their volume,
the words they have started or stopped using — and the player infers.
*"They answer short, flatter than before, and they have gone formal on
you."* Not *"the Military is now feeling neutral."*

*(That line was a debug readout promoted to a mechanic. It is gone.
`dispositionTag()` survives as an internal convenience for the sim and
its tests, marked never-show-the-player.)*

**The uncertainty is structural, not noise.** Cues derive from the
VISIBLE level — internal feeling times Social Filter — so:

> a customer at **−80 behind a tight lid** and one at **−28 with no lid
> at all** produce *word-for-word identical* cues.

The player cannot tell them apart, which is precisely what "the lid on
the pot" was always supposed to mean. Reading people is a skill because
the evidence genuinely underdetermines the answer.

Cues can be read per axis, so a caller can be warm toward the agent and
cold about the Co-Op at once — and *show* both.

### The presented category is the customer's guess — locked

**A customer reports a symptom, not a diagnosis, and the category they
imply is frequently wrong.** Ask how many genuinely different reasons
there are for *"I forgot my password"*: a typo, caps lock, a stale
autofill, a recent change they forgot, somebody else changing it, a
migrated account, an expired credential, MFA failing rather than the
password, a locked account, a suspension for non-payment, or no account
on this system at all. **Several of those are not password problems.**

So a root cause may sit **outside** the category it arrives under. This
is what makes Classify a judgement rather than a label, and it is the
same shape as "Pigs is Pigs" (see "Screening & Classification") — rigid
classification meeting a reality that does not fit it.

**Built, for Phantom Inventory.** Six root causes, sharing one symptom
and one menu of investigation actions, each supplying its own evidence:

| Root cause | What it actually is |
| --- | --- |
| Unlogged sale | Phantom Inventory |
| Split lot, duplicated stamp | Phantom Inventory |
| Receipt never posted | Phantom Inventory |
| Second leg still in transit | **Bulk Potion Order Delay** |
| Misfiled at intake | **Storefront Listing Error** |
| Constabulary hold | **no category covers this yet** |

**About 42% of Phantom Inventory calls are not Phantom Inventory.**
Filing it right requires working out what actually happened, and the
same diagnosis is correct on one call and wrong on the next — there is
no globally right answer for a category, only a right answer for *this*
call.

*Content gap surfaced by this work*: "Goods Under Hold" has no category
anywhere in `TICKET_TEMPLATES`. Left unnamed and unbuilt rather than
forced into an existing category that does not fit.

## Character Disposition Model — Baggage, Sensitivity, Social Filter

Design intent, not yet built — but foundational enough that other
systems (multi-angle recurring characters, `recallMemory()`) should be
built on top of this, not before it.

**The problem this solves.** Right now `gameLogic.js` gives every
customer an identical starting disposition (0) and an identical
reaction to a given response quality (the same `RESPONSE_QUALITY`
delta applies to everyone). Two brand-new customers of the same
archetype are mechanically indistinguishable until the player treats
them differently. This model replaces that flatness with three
independent per-customer traits:

1. **Baggage** — a starting disposition offset. Not every customer
   starts at 0; some walk in already carrying something, before the
   player's said a word — what they bring from past guilds, past bad
   service, past products or subscriptions, even past dealings with
   the Co-Op itself.
2. **Sensitivity** — a multiplier on how much a given interaction's
   raw effect actually registers internally for this customer. Same
   response quality, different customers, different-sized reaction.
3. **Social Filter** — a dampener applied *after* Sensitivity scales
   the raw effect, governing how much of that internal reaction
   actually shows up as a visible disposition swing. This is the
   missing piece that keeps a character from swinging wildly between
   extremes on every single interaction — the lid on the pot, the
   valve on a pressure cooker. Without it, high sensitivity alone
   means the full scaled effect always shows, every time.

**Pipeline, in order**: raw response-quality delta → scaled by
Sensitivity → dampened by Social Filter → applied to disposition.

### Disposition is a vector, not a number — locked

`gameLogic.js` currently stores one `disposition` integer per customer,
and the three traits above were originally specced to modulate that one
number. That is not enough to hold the most ordinary outcome in support
work: **"you've been lovely, your company is garbage."** A scalar
cannot represent a customer who thinks well of the agent, badly of TLC,
and is exhausted by the whole affair regardless.

**Four quantities per customer. Three are feelings; one is not.**

| Quantity | What it tracks | How the player reads it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **You** | how they feel about *this agent* | tone toward the player specifically; and, late and filtered, the survey |
| **TLC** | how they feel about *the company* | what they say about the Co-Op, unprompted |
| **The Experience** | **the whole experience that led to contact**, and the contact itself | **how they explain their request** — anger, frustration, terseness, going quiet |
| **Understanding** | whether they actually grasp what happened. **Not a feeling.** | **fewer calls about similar requests** later; or more confusion now |

**The Experience is broader than TLC's treatment of them.** It is not
just hold time and transfers — it is everything that led to the contact,
including whatever went wrong before TLC ever picked up. Forty potions
missing before a festival *is* the experience, and the customer arrives
already carrying it. This is why the axis is legible in the **opening
statement and in how they answer questions**, not in a meter: a
frustrating experience comes through as anger, or frustration, or
silence. Reading it is the Requestor post doing its work.

*(Distinct from Baggage, which is what they carry in from past dealings.
The Experience is this situation. A customer can have clean Baggage and
a terrible Experience, or the reverse.)*

**Understanding's tells run in both directions** and both are delayed or
indirect: it shows as **customers calling about similar requests less**
over time, or as **confusion, more**, right now. Neither is a readout.

### Memory is multifaceted — locked

`recallMemory()` today distorts three things independently: **what** the
ticket was about, **when** it happened, and **who** handled it. That
shape is right and the count is short. Memory has **five facets**, each
able to fade or distort on its own:

| Facet | What a customer misremembers |
| --- | --- |
| **Experiences** | what happened to them, and when |
| **Company** | what the Co-Op did, or is like, or did to them before |
| **Staff** | who helped — the agent, or somebody else entirely |
| **Products/Services** | what was actually shipped, ordered, charged, or promised |
| **Emotional** | how they felt about it, which decays on its own schedule |

Three of the five are already built in some form — `category` maps onto
Products/Services, the timing distortion onto Experiences, and
`agentAttribution` onto Staff (the existing decoy-name corruption). The
two missing are **Company** and **Emotional**.

**Emotional is the one that changes the most.** It is the facet that
lets a customer remember an interaction as worse or better than it was,
independently of remembering the facts correctly — the thing that makes
"I know you fixed it but I still felt awful about that call" possible.
It should decay on its own curve, not the factual one.

**Which facet distorts depends on what the memory is about.** There is
no single axis memory "rides on" — a recollection about an agent decays
on **Staff**, one about the Co-Op on **Company**, one about what was in
the crate on **Products/Services**. The Emotional facet runs alongside
all of them on its own schedule, which is why someone can recall the
facts correctly and the feeling wrongly, or the reverse.

**Do not wire these one-to-one onto the disposition axes.** They are
adjacent and not identical — Staff is broader than the **You** axis
(it covers other agents by name), Company is broader than **TLC** (it
covers what the Co-Op is *like*, not just this account's standing), and
Emotional cuts across all of them. See *Inputs spill across axes* below
for the same caution applied to the other direction.

### Inputs spill across axes — locked

**There is no one-to-one mapping from input to axis.** An earlier draft
of this section claimed each fencepost owned exactly one output and
called the tidiness evidence the model was sound. That was backwards —
the tidiness was the tell that it had been over-designed. Corrected:

- **Correctness** (Requestor, Investigation, Support) **moves all three
  feeling axes.** Getting it right is a fact about the company, about
  the agent who did it, and about how the episode went.
- **Operations** (hold, transfers, repeats, question load) **also moves
  all three.** A company that makes you wait is a worse company; an
  agent who makes you repeat yourself is a worse agent; and the wait
  itself becomes part of the experience they arrived with.
- **Sympathy/Empathy is a soft skill and behaves like one** — it
  **primarily** moves **You**, and can move TLC and The Experience as
  well. Warmth from one agent colours the whole company for some
  callers, and a hard call handled kindly is remembered as a better
  experience than it was.
- **Education** primarily moves **Understanding**, with its failure mode
  landing on **You** (see below).

**Why this matters mechanically, not just for realism**: a one-to-one
mapping would be learnable in an afternoon. Players would read "empathy
raises the You bar" and optimise the axis they wanted. Spillover means
no single input is a lever on a single number, which is what keeps the
five posts from degenerating into five sliders — the same failure as the
original four universal response buttons, one level up.

*Weights are not specified here.* Which axis a given input moves *most*
is a tuning question, and only Empathy's primary (You) is established.
Do not invent a weighting table and treat it as locked.

This also gives "Running the Desk" a customer-facing consequence it
previously lacked: queue and pace stop being pure player-side texture
and start landing on somebody.

**The Experience axis is Customer Effort**, the third leg of real
contact-center measurement alongside satisfaction-with-agent and
satisfaction-with-company — it exists in the industry precisely because
the other two miss it. Its most interesting property here: **thorough
investigation is good for TLC and bad for the Experience.** Asking all
five questions gets the right answer *and* makes the caller sit through
five questions. The candle therefore does double duty — it is the
player's budget and the caller's patience at the same time, and
spending it is never purely virtuous.

*(This closes the open question previously logged under "Running the
Desk" about what hold time feeds. It feeds the Experience axis
directly — not Baggage, and not a separate wrinkle.)*

**What this does to the three traits above:**
- **Baggage becomes per-axis.** A first-time caller can walk in already
  hating TLC while holding no opinion of *you* at all — they have never
  spoken to you. That falls out naturally and is worth preserving:
  Baggage on **You** should start near zero for a stranger and carry
  over only for a returning customer, while Baggage on **TLC** may be
  loaded from the first word.
- **Sensitivity and Social Filter stay scalar** — they are traits of the
  person, not of a relationship. One lid, one valve, applied to whatever
  is moving.

**Cozy check**: four numbers per customer is an *internal* model, not a
UI. The player must never see four bars. Per "Running the Desk," this
surfaces as behavior, dialogue, and secondhand remarks — a caller who
thanks you and still asks for your supervisor is the vector doing its
job without a single figure on screen.

**All three are fully individual — archetype is never a formula for
any of them.** Two customers of the same archetype can have wildly
different Baggage, Sensitivity, and Social Filter values, and
archetype tells you nothing reliable about which you're getting. This
is the direct mechanical backing for "archetype rhyme, not archetype
lookup" (Customer Service Philosophy) — that principle has been prose
until now; this is what actually implements it. Grounding given for
Social Filter specifically: neighbors describing a serial killer as
"he seemed like such a nice, quiet man" — a filter can mask something
*completely* different from what it presents, not just soften what's
actually there. A player can't reliably infer the truth from the
presentation, no matter how well they think they're reading someone.

**Contextual "wrinkles" are allowed — archetype-level formulas are
not.** Specific, legible facts about a *particular customer's
situation* can bias (never determine) their individual trait draws.
Examples given: COG affiliation might skew Baggage toward the guarded
end (more likely, not certain, to start wary); an Adventurer just back
from a successful expedition vs. one about to depart might skew
Sensitivity or Social Filter differently (relief vs. keyed-up
anxiety). The line that matters: these are facts about *this
customer's circumstances*, not their broad archetype category —
"COG-affiliated" and "just back from an adventure" are situations, not
archetypes, and they bias a random draw rather than replacing it with
a lookup table.

## Education & Empathy — Advanced Skills, Earned

**The problem they solve.** Of the five fenceposts, three (Requestor,
Investigation, Support) are what a competent new agent does on day one.
Education and Sympathy/Empathy are not day-one skills, and pretending
otherwise produces a seven-surface checklist — exactly the rigidity that
made the original four universal response buttons worthless.

**Locked: both are advanced skills the player earns.** The early game is
a genuine three-post call — get the facts, fix the thing. That is not a
stripped-down tutorial version of the job; it is an accurate portrait of
a new agent. Correct and cold. The growth arc is learning to be correct
*and* human, which is the real arc of the first years on a phone, and
it gives a cozy roguelite the meta-progression the genre expects and
this design previously had none of.

### Education — translating jargon into plain language

Not "give them information." Specifically: **converting corporate and
operational jargon into terms the customer actually holds.** Success
leaves them feeling they learned something. Failure leaves them feeling
**talked down to**.

**The asymmetry is the whole design, and it is why this is advanced:**

- **Success** raises **Understanding** — abstract, delayed, largely
  invisible in the moment.
- **Failure** lowers **You** — immediate, personal, aimed straight at
  the agent.

The risk lands on the player; the reward does not. That is exactly why
real agents skip it under pressure, and why a novice avoiding Education
is making a *correct* call for their skill level. The game should not
punish that early — it should let the player discover later that the
move exists and is worth the risk.

Understanding is also what suppresses the repeat contact, wiring
Education directly into the reopen-risk machinery already built in
`resolveTicket()` and `endShift()` — a system that currently runs off a
generic correctness scalar because the component that should drive it
did not exist.

### Sympathy/Empathy — moving how they feel about you

A **soft skill**, and it behaves like one. It moves **You**
primarily — kindness, support, understanding and respect land there, in
both directions — but it is not confined to that axis: warmth from a
single agent colours some callers' view of TLC entirely, and a hard call
handled kindly is remembered as a better experience than it actually
was. Unlike Education it has no separate abstract payoff; its effect is
the relationship, spilling outward from it.

### Unlocking — the mechanism already exists

**Do not invent a skill tree.** Two Figurehead policy levers are already
locked in "Leadership & Governance":

- *More coaching meetings between agents and managers*
- *Additional mandatory training*

Both currently read as bureaucratic weather — things done *to* the
player. Make them the delivery mechanism and two systems pay off at
once: Phase 7's **Coaching** register gains something real to hand over
instead of only relaying Figurehead policy, and *mandatory training*
stops being a joke about corporate overhead and becomes the thing the
player actually wants. A Figurehead who mandates training is now a good
administration for the player's development and a drag on their
throughput — precisely the tension the Conclave material exists to
create.

### The unlock grants reliability, not permission — locked

**"You have not unlocked Empathy yet" is the wrong message for a cozy
game.** It reads as the game forbidding kindness.

The player may **always attempt** either skill. What the unlock changes
is **variance and visibility**: a day-one agent trying to be kind to an
upset caller is clumsy, lands it sometimes, misfires sometimes, and
cannot tell in advance which. Earning the skill narrows the spread and
shows the player what they are actually doing. This matches the true
distinction — the *instinct* is basic, the *skill* is advanced — and it
gives a Coaching meeting something to be about that is not a menu item:
a supervisor explaining why the thing you tried last Tuesday landed
badly.

### Unlocks persist across runs — locked

**The player grows.** This is a cozy roguelite **RPG**, and skills once
earned stay earned into the next run. The fiction is one career getting
better at a job, not a series of unrelated first days.

Two consequences worth stating now, before anything is built:

- **The three-post early game is a phase the player leaves permanently**,
  not a difficulty setting they revisit. It should be written as a real
  stage of a career with its own texture — the stretch where you are
  correct and cold and do not yet know what you are missing — rather
  than as a locked-down tutorial to be endured.
- **Run-scoped state and career-scoped state are now different things.**
  Reputation, the candle, the day's queue, and a given customer's
  disposition vector belong to a run. Earned skills do not. Nothing in
  `gameLogic.js` currently distinguishes these, and whatever persistence
  layer gets built needs the split from the start — retrofitting it
  later means untangling every piece of state at once.

Not built — design only. See "Character Disposition Model" for the axes
these move, and "Open Questions" for what is still undecided.

## Multi-Angle Recurring Characters

Builds directly on the Character Disposition Model above — designed
to sit on top of it, not independently.

**Symmetric, locked.** Either party in a linked pair could be the one
whose ticket generates first; there's no fixed direction (not "only a
Villain can spawn their nemesis"). Purely a function of which ticket
happens to come up first.

**Who can name-check whom — broader than one adversarial pair.** Not
limited to Hero/Villain nemesis pairs. An NPC can name-check other
NPCs. Henchmen can name-check the Villain they work for. Partners can
name-check the customers they serve. And — a distinct case, below —
TLC's own employees can name-check the people they're calling about.

**Two genuinely different distortion types, not one:**

1. **Adversarial distortion** (the original Hero/Villain case) — two
   parties with opposing self-interest, each truthfully reporting
   their own biased perspective. Neither is lying; per "Villain is
   the hero of their own story" (Logistics & Shipping Tiers), they
   just see the same event differently. Motivated bias.
2. **Proxy distortion** (new) — a third party speaking *on behalf of*
   someone else, with no motive to distort anything at all. **TLC
   salespeople contacting support on behalf of a customer or partner**
   is the driving example: they want to help, genuinely — and the
   good intent is exactly what makes it worse. It removes the actual
   affected party from the conversation, making the truth *harder*
   to reach, not because anyone's lying, but because the account is
   structurally secondhand. The agent may need to work to get past
   the intermediary to reach whoever was actually affected. This is
   also a realistic, non-invented dynamic — sales-vs-support friction
   inside a real company, not a contrivance.

**Mechanically**: when a ticket's dialogue names a specific other
person, that person becomes a real customer entity in the roster —
`id`, `tier`, `archetype`, and their own independently-rolled Baggage/
Sensitivity/Social Filter (not inherited from whoever named them).
The two linked customers share a tagged connection (who they're
linked to, which incident connects them), but their dispositions stay
fully separate. Scheduling rides the same shape as `pendingFollowUps`
(holds/disputes already work this way): seed a "linked customer
surfaces later" entry when the first ticket resolves, let it come due
in a future shift the same way a hold or reopen does.

Not built — design only, see Open Questions.

## Partnership & Onboarding Inquiries

**The call center has zero sway over the *outcome* — locked.** Agents
happen to share a contact number with the application process by
administrative coincidence, not because support has any real
authority over approval or rejection. But the agent's *own part* of
the interaction — explaining the process and submitting a request on
the caller's behalf — is a real, completable task. `quality` applies
as usual to how well that's handled.

**The process, locked:**
1. Applicant fills out an application.
2. Reviewed by the **Partner Operations Selection Team ("POST")** —
   a distinct internal TLC department, first mention here, separate
   from the Research division. "POST" fits the naming methodology
   already established, and does it doubly: "post" is the literal
   real word both for submitting something for review *and* for mail
   delivery itself (postal service, "to post" a letter) — the
   department that decides who gets to become a delivery partner is
   named after delivery, not just review. Two true meanings stacking,
   same pattern as TLC, POOF, COG, and the Village of Import.
3. Final approval rests with the same 7-seat Leadership Council (2
   permanent guildmaster seats + 5 elected) that also elects the
   Figurehead — see Leadership & Governance. The same coalition-
   building dynamic that governs any other Council decision plausibly
   applies here too, though that's not stated outright.

*(Observation, not locked: there's a real comic mismatch between a
board built for grand policy and ceremonial Figurehead elections also
being the final sign-off on something as mundane as a tavern's
delivery-partner application. Fits the "gums up the works" theme
already established elsewhere, but this is a read on the implications,
not a stated fact.)*

**Ticket shape, corrected.** There is no phone infrastructure
connecting the call center to POST — `transfer` was never actually
available here, since that requires something to hand off a live call
*to*. The right shape: the agent explains the process and submits a
request to POST for follow-up on the caller's behalf, and that
submission itself *is* the resolution — **`resolved` is the normal,
correct status**, from the call center's side. The application's
actual outcome (approval or not) is separate, happens later, and
doesn't block this ticket closing now. `hold` may still fit a later,
different ticket — an applicant calling back to check status, with
genuinely nothing new to report. Not yet built.

## Meetings with Supervisors & Coworkers

Builds on three things already locked: the Character Disposition
Model, the Figurehead's standing-policy layer (which has had no actual
in-game delivery mechanism until now), and the folk-wisdom-vs-
official-doctrine tension from Customer Service Philosophy below.

**Three registers, three different weights** — unchanged from the
original sketch, now with real mechanical backing instead of being
purely narrative:

1. **Feedback** (Supervisor-delivered) — references specifics from
   the player's last shift summary (tag counts, status counts, any
   disputes). Same "cite something specific vs. carry a vague tone"
   split already built for `recallMemory()` — but applied reflexively
   here, to the *supervisor's own* disposition toward the player,
   tracked the same way customer disposition is (via the Character
   Disposition Model — supervisors get their own Baggage, Sensitivity,
   and Social Filter too, same as any customer). A supervisor who's
   built up warm disposition is more likely to cite something specific
   and complimentary; one who's soured, something specific and
   critical, or just a cold general tone. Purely narrative in a first
   pass — no stat changes, just flavor grounded in a real, tracked
   number instead of randomness.
2. **Coaching** (Supervisor-delivered), two distinct triggers:
   - **Rough-patch-triggered** — fires reliably (not just a random
     roll) when real conditions are met: 2+ disputes in the last
     shift, or reputation below a threshold. Could carry a small,
     temporary mechanical effect for the next shift or two (e.g., a
     reduced `DISPUTE_CHANCE_BY_QUALITY` while the tip is still fresh).
   - **Figurehead-policy-triggered** — guaranteed on the first lobby
     visit after a new Figurehead takes office. This is the actual
     delivery mechanism for the standing-policy layer (more coaching
     required, pace pressure, specialist/generalist flip, procedure
     updates) that's existed as an abstract design note since
     Leadership & Governance — without this, that system has no way
     to actually reach the player.
   - Same supervisor-disposition texture as Feedback applies: identical
     official content can land very differently depending on the
     specific supervisor's own rolled traits — a sympathetic supervisor
     might soften bad news, an unsympathetic one might deliver the
     exact same policy coldly.
3. **"Just to chat"** (Coworker-delivered, not a supervisor) — no
   mechanical effect at all, pure relationship texture. This is where
   the folk-wisdom-vs-official-doctrine tension actually plays out
   narratively: a coworker passing down "History Rhymes," "Trust and
   Verify," or "Eliminate the Impossible" the way a veteran actually
   would — unofficial, unwritten — in contrast to whatever official
   doctrine Leadership hands down through Supervisor-delivered
   Coaching.

**Supervisor vs. Coworker is a role, not a fixed cast.** Official
content (Feedback, Coaching) comes from whoever's currently playing
supervisor; unofficial content (Chat) comes from a peer. Draws from
the same small named roster that already exists in code —
`DECOY_AGENT_NAMES` (Cassidy, Thistle, Bram, Pell, Wren), currently
used only for misremembered-agent-name corruption in `recallMemory()`
— now with actual assigned roles instead of being purely decorative.

**Mechanically, proposed shape** (unchanged from the original sketch):
a `rollBreakRoomEncounter(lastShiftSummary, figureheadJustChanged, rng)`
function, called from the lobby phase. Returns `null` most of the
time for routine chances; near-certain for rough-patch coaching when
conditions are met; guaranteed for Figurehead-policy coaching right
after a new Figurehead takes office.

Not built — design only.

## Customer Service Philosophy — Frontline Folk Wisdom

**Framing** (proposed, not explicitly confirmed or rejected): this is
*not* official Leadership doctrine — Leadership's real policy is
probably more literal and scripted, "a pig is a pig" energy. This is
unofficial wisdom passed frontline-to-frontline, surviving despite
Leadership, not because of it. Sets up a natural tension for the
meetings/coaching system.

*(Note: these three principles are adapted from Phil's own stated
real-world customer-service philosophy — see
`/topics/customer-service-philosophy.md`.)*

*(Structural note: these principles sit on top of the five fenceposts
— see "The Five Fenceposts — What Every Call Is Made Of." "Trust and
Verify" describes the seam between **Request/Requestor** and
**Investigation**; "Eliminate the impossible, then Occam's Razor" is
**Support** — the scientific method to solutions — stated in
frontline language; "History doesn't repeat itself, it can rhyme
though" is how a **Requestor** gets read fast, off pattern rather
than off a script. Notably, **Education** and **Sympathy/Empathy**
have no folk principle of their own here, and no representation in
code either. That gap is consistent across both, which is part of why
the fencepost model is trusted rather than merely accepted.)*

### 1. "History doesn't repeat itself, it can rhyme though"
Multiple layers, not one idea:
- **Reading a returning customer** — their new ticket won't match the
  old one exactly, but the shape is familiar. (This is a plainspoken
  description of how `recallMemory()` already works in code — pattern,
  not rerun.)
- **Player learning across tickets** — players build pattern
  recognition across *different* customers and requests that aren't
  identical but share a shape. Handling one well informs the next.
  A player-skill dimension, separate from any single customer's
  memory of the player.
- **Archetype rhyme, not archetype lookup** — customer "types" blend
  traits, the way real personality typing does (most people read as
  more than one type). A tactic that works on one Villain won't
  automatically work on the next Villain — and the same holds across
  Heroes, Sidekicks, NPCs, anti-heroes, anti-villains, henchmen, and
  every other archetype. Caution against treating archetype as a
  script. Concretely: the archetype label shapes a character's
  emotional stability, knowledge, and reaction to unwanted answers,
  alongside every other trait they have, and those traits influence
  each other rather than just stacking — the same way an RPG class
  doesn't fully determine a build. A Mage with low mana isn't casting
  a massive fireball, they're throwing baseball-sized ones — but
  points dumped into accuracy and power make that small fireball
  genuinely effective at precise targets, where the "massive fireball"
  build is more fire-and-pray. Same class, same archetype label,
  different character. (See "Villain vs. restricted criminal" under
  Logistics & Shipping Tiers for the policy-vs-character distinction
  this depends on.) (Related material already exists in Phil's MBTI
  Customer Training System project — not merged, just adjacent.)
- **Day-to-day variance, same person** — even the most loyal,
  best-known customer isn't identical day to day. History isn't
  "solved." Perspective and context shift daily; autopilot on a
  favorite customer is still a mistake. (Partial mechanical foothold
  already exists — shift modifiers affect everyone's day, including
  known customers — but this doesn't fully cover per-individual daily
  variance yet.)

### 2. "Trust and Verify"
Trust the customer's experience, verify what happened. Trust the
experience they lived, verify the outcome. Trust the knowledge,
verify the solution. A two-step instinct — trust what the customer
says happened to them, but verify it against the record before acting
— distinct from blind trust (gets you burned) or reflexive suspicion
(bad service). Proposed as a future two-beat dialogue mechanic; not
yet built.

### 3. "Eliminate the impossible, then Occam's Razor"
When you've ruled out the impossible, whatever remains — however
improbable — must be the truth. The advanced diagnostic move, for
tickets that don't fit a known category and scripted responses don't
apply.

## Naming Methodology (apply this to everything still unnamed)

Established the hard way, worth keeping as a standing rule rather
than relearning it each time:

**The failure mode**: inventing a name top-down, for effect — jamming
one corporate-sounding word against one medieval/fantasy-sounding word
(e.g. "Wyrdline," "Chartered Guilds" as originally proposed, "Charter
Audit," "GUILD Ceremony"). This is what an outsider does — someone
naming a thing without living in the world it belongs to.

**The actual pattern**, per the reference points given (Tolkien, GoT,
The Wandering Inn, Dungeon Crawler Carl for fantasy voice; Disney,
Pixar, Marvel for tone): a good name is **derived**, not invented —
from a place, a founder, a material, or a historical event. Take away
the thing it's derived from and the name stops making sense. "The
Iron Bank of Braavos" isn't a pun; it's a bank, backed by iron
credibility, in a city called Braavos.

**Validated example**: "The Conclave of Operations & Policy" —
reusing a real, existing word ("conclave") correctly for what it
actually describes, tied directly to the user's own papal-conclave
analogy. Not invented vocabulary — borrowed, correctly.

**Everything named so far has now gone through this treatment**: the
company's own name, "The Coalition of Guilds," and the shared town —
see "The Company Name," "The Coalition of Guilds," and "The Merger &
Founding." What's left unnamed (the Realm, the Chartered/COG member
guilds individually) simply hasn't come up yet, not skipped over.

**A separate, related rule for ticket** `category` **names
specifically**: this field is a classification, not dialogue. A good
category name is a *term of art* for a real problem type the customer
experiences — not the customer's own narrative complaint (that's what
`blurb` is for), and not an internal mechanism the customer never sees
or experiences at all. "Phantom Inventory" is the validated example: a
real retail/logistics term, borrowed correctly, that a customer would
recognize as *their* situation without ever using that phrase
themselves. Two distinct failure modes to watch for, both drawn from
rejected attempts: (1) dressing up the customer's own complaint in
flavor language ("The Caravan Hasn't Come" — that's what the customer
would *say*, not a category classifying it) and (2) naming the
internal cause instead of the customer-facing problem ("Misfiled with
POST" — a customer never experiences their own paperwork status, only
its downstream effect).

## Open Questions — Not Yet Established

Listed, not guessed at:

- **Diagnosis options visible before investigating (Phantom
  Inventory).** A player could read all four diagnosis labels cold and
  blind-guess the correct-sounding one without investigating anything.
  Blunted, not solved: a correct guess without the key clue caps at
  `adequate`, never `correct` (tested), but the option wording itself
  still carries information it probably shouldn't. Possible fixes not
  yet decided: vaguer diagnosis wording, gating diagnosis options
  behind at least one investigation action, or accepting this as a
  reasonable tradeoff for how small Phase 0 is scoped.
- **Running the desk — operational layer.** Now written (see "Running
  the Desk — Queue, Pace, and Being Measured"); nothing built. Four
  things are locked there: the blended live/async queue, all four
  measurement axes with permission to disagree with each other,
  escalation running both sideways and upward, and the absence of any
  knowledge base. Several things inside it are deliberately still open:
  whether hold time feeds disposition (and via Baggage or a separate
  wrinkle); whether the player ever sees raw metrics or only hears them
  secondhand through meetings; and the names of the sibling teams and
  the upward escalation tier, left unnamed rather than invented per
  "Naming Methodology."
- **How the five fenceposts and the five phases wire together.**
  Largely settled. Both exist, both are non-linear, they are different
  axes (phases are interface surfaces, posts are what the call is made
  of), and each post moves the disposition vector — with **spill across
  axes, not one-to-one ownership**; see "Character Disposition Model,"
  *Inputs spill across axes*. A call can be complete on
  all five phases and still be missing posts; that gap is the
  difference between a call that was *processed* and one that was
  *handled*, and it is the thing to design toward.
  **Locked**: Education and Empathy **ride on the existing five
  surfaces**, as a quality of *how* the player asks and answers. They do
  not get surfaces of their own — they are not places you go, which is
  also what keeps a call from becoming a seven-item checklist.
- **What the player is shown now that disposition is a vector.** The
  biggest unresolved consequence of the vector, and three questions
  wearing one coat.

  *The mechanical situation*: `dispositionTag(score)` takes one integer
  and returns one of five words (hostile / frustrated / neutral / warm /
  loyal). It is consumed in four places — the outcome object from
  `resolveTicket()`; the post-call feedback line ("The Potion Shop is
  now feeling neutral"); `endShift()`'s `tagCounts`, which drives the
  shift summary; and the design handoff's 12-segment STANDING meter on
  the Account Record. Every one of those now has to answer *which
  quantity am I reporting*.

  *Two answers that look obvious and are both wrong.* **Averaging the
  axes into one number** destroys the exact state the vector exists to
  represent — "lovely agent, garbage company" averages to neutral.
  **Showing all four** breaks the cozy constraint locked in "Running the
  Desk" and turns a customer into a stat block, which is the opposite of
  a game about reading people.

  *Current lean — give each axis a home that already exists*, rather
  than building new widgets:
  - **STANDING meter → TLC.** An account standing *is* a company-level
    record; that is what the screen already is. A precise institutional
    figure is also fine on cozy grounds — it is a bureaucratic artifact,
    not a feelings gauge.
  - **Post-call feedback line → You.** How the interaction with *this
    agent* went is the player's immediate feedback.
  - **The Experience → no readout at all.** It surfaces as behaviour
    *during* the call — impatience, terseness, "is this going to take
    much longer" — which also makes it the feedback channel for hold
    time and question load.
  - **Understanding → no readout either.** It shows up later, as whether
    they call back and how they open the next call. A delayed, indirect
    tell matches Education's asymmetry.

  **Resolved.** The tag is gone from the player-facing path entirely —
  see "Reading a customer," above. The player gets observable cues and
  infers. There is no phrase to author because there is no verdict to
  deliver.
- **The five fenceposts — the call's anatomy.** Now written (see "The
  Five Fenceposts — What Every Call Is Made Of"); nothing built. The
  model itself is locked: five components on every call, placed as a
  layout rather than run as a sequence, with order read off the ground
  and gating a property of the caller rather than a rule in the
  system. What's open is all mechanical: how a post registers as
  "set," whether posts score independently or interact, and how any of
  it reaches the player without becoming the checklist the model
  explicitly isn't.
- **Can the player write a dishonest note?** The record is a claim
  about the fence, not a readout of it — people lie, pad, and
  self-flatter. Whether the *player* gets that option is undecided,
  and it's a cozy-tone question as much as a mechanical one: the
  natural consequence is self-inflicted and delayed (you're the one
  reading that note three shifts later), which is cozy-compatible, but
  it needs deciding rather than assuming.
- **Are notes amendable once the truth is known?** When a player
  returns to a customer and now knows the answer they got wrong three
  shifts ago, does the original note stand as written, or can it be
  corrected? And does putting it right clear the customer's memory of
  the wrong answer, or only add to it? The repair call is the most
  valuable scenario the fencepost model produces, and these two
  decisions determine its shape.
- **Two accounts of the same call — built.** Resolved from the earlier
  proposal, which was wrong in an instructive way. The first version had
  the player's case notes *becoming* the customer's memory, which
  quietly turns TLC's paperwork into somebody's mind. **They are two
  separate memories.** The customer's is grounded in what happened *to
  them* and owes nothing to our records; the company's is the note, only
  ever as good as what a busy agent had time to write. The agent reads
  one and talks to the other, and neither side is lying.

  **The contradiction surfaces where one account diverges from the
  other** — not as a side-by-side comparison the player consults.
  `accountDivergences()` raises an event only at points of actual
  conflict, per facet, and stays silent while the two agree, which is
  most calls. That is "Trust and Verify" in mechanical form: trust the
  experience they lived, verify it against the record before acting.

  Still open: whether a thin note should make the *next* agent's job
  harder in some way beyond having less to go on.
- **Player-written notes as `recallMemory()`'s source.** Superseded by
  the entry above; kept for the reasoning. Still open,
  now reframed: not "wrap-up as a cost" (that framing is superseded —
  see "Between-call work, and what the record actually is") but "the
  record is a third imperfect account alongside what happened and what
  the customer remembers." Remains a real change to `recallMemory()`'s
  inputs, which today reads a `history` array the sim writes
  automatically.
- **Who actually administers Realm law day-to-day.** The ruler
  ("The Unseen") is absent from public life and may not even be
  confirmed alive — so *someone* still has to issue proclamations and
  run the restricted-goods list TLC complies with. Council? Ministry?
  Something else? Genuinely open, not guessed at here.
- **Node vs. Courier ticket-category mapping** — the naming and role
  distinction is locked (see "The Merger & Founding"), but which
  *future* Partner ticket categories belong to Node vs. Courier isn't
  spec'd, and whether a single business holding both roles at once
  needs its own handling is undecided.
- **Character Disposition Model (Baggage/Sensitivity/Social Filter)**
  — fully designed (see its own section above), not built. Needs
  actual value ranges and distribution shapes before it can be coded;
  `recallMemory()` and multi-angle recurring characters should be
  built on top of this once it exists, not before.
- **Classification-dispute ticket category** — "Pigs is Pigs" logic
  is designed but not built into `gameLogic.js` yet.
- **Figurehead standing-policy system** — design intent only (a
  persistent layer above `SHIFT_MODIFIERS`); no code yet.
- **Network-growth instability** — a location-specific, multi-shift
  volume spike for newly-connected towns/outposts, distinct from
  `SHIFT_MODIFIERS`. Design intent only, no code yet.
- **Wildline routing — the one remaining candidate tier.** Priority
  Relay and Caravan are now locked (see Logistics & Shipping Tiers) —
  Wildline routing is the only shipping tier still undecided, since
  it's the only one that needs a Research division breakthrough that
  hasn't happened yet (riding a wild, un-bent line — fast, genuinely
  unpredictable). Not locked or built.
- **Wildline instability as a distinct ticket-content source.**
  Separate from the mundane instability already tied to bent
  trade-route lines (which causes logistics glitches — delays,
  misroutes): a genuinely wild, un-bent line could actually
  *transform cargo in transit*, not just misroute it. Concrete
  example given: a boy ships his adventuring father a Small Healing
  Potion; it arrives as a Large Healing Potion (with a size-upcharge
  fee neither of them agreed to), or empty, or altered some other
  way. Rhymes directly with the "Pigs is Pigs" classification-dispute
  theme — same rigid-billing-meets-reality mismatch, except the cargo
  actually changed instead of a clerk misjudging it. Tied to wildline
  routing above; would give that tier its own distinct ticket flavor
  (transformation problems, not logistics problems) once built.
  Design intent only, no code yet.
- **Multi-Angle Recurring Characters** — fully designed (see its own
  section above: symmetric linking, two distortion types, scheduling
  shape). Not built — needs to wait on the Character Disposition
  Model existing in code first, same dependency as before.
- **Organizational-account memory asymmetry** — the caller for a
  Business-tier account (or COG) may not know their
  own account's history, even though the agent/company records do.
  `recallMemory()` doesn't currently distinguish individual customers
  from organizational ones. No mechanic for this yet.
- **Meetings with Supervisors & Coworkers** — fully designed (see its
  own section above: three registers, supervisor/coworker disposition
  reuse, and the actual delivery mechanism for Figurehead policy).
  Not built yet.
- **Partnership & Onboarding Inquiries** — fully designed (see its own
  section above: zero sway over the outcome, the POST/Council pipeline,
  and why `resolved` — not `transfer` — is the normal correct status).
  Not built yet.
